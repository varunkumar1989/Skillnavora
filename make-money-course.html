Module 1: The Arbitrage Mindset and High-Margin Offer Selection

Chapter 1.1: Escaping the tutorial trap: the 80/20 sales-to-tech allocation

Video: 00:00–02:40

1. Core objective. Build a weekly time plan that puts sales activity ahead of learning, and stop adding new AI tools until you have a paying client.

2. Key concepts and mental models - The tutorial trap. The video describes a loop: learn a new tool, feel behind, learn another. The loop feels productive and produces no revenue. [Opinion] - The 80/20 split. The video recommends spending about 20% of time learning AI (enough to show samples and talk about it at a high level) and about 80% on sales: building lists, writing offers, reaching out and running calls. [Opinion] - Credibility through output. The video's argument is that a business buys proof of results, not a list of tools you have studied. [Opinion] - Action beats preparation. A small number of outreach messages sent this week teaches more than another month of study. [Opinion]

Key terms in this chapter

Term Definition
LLM (large language model) An AI system trained on very large amounts of text to predict and generate language. Claude, ChatGPT and Grok are examples.
Tutorial trap Spending time on learning that feels like progress but does not create a client or revenue.
Outbound Contacting prospects first (for example by LinkedIn message or email) rather than waiting for them to find you.
Pipeline The list of prospects at each stage, from contacted to meeting booked to client.

3. Step-by-step SOP checklist - [ ] Write your weekly calendar with two blocks: about 2 hours of learning and about 8 hours of sales activity (contacting, follow-up, calls, offer and asset work). This is the video's ratio applied to a 10-hour week. [Estimate] - [ ] List every AI tool you are currently learning. Keep three. Pause the rest until you have a client. - [ ] Set one number for the week: for example, 50 new outreach messages sent (Chapter 3.1 explains where that number comes from). - [ ] Each Friday, record how many messages you sent, replies and calls booked. Do not record "hours learned" as progress.

4. Objections and risk mitigation - "I need to know more before I sell." Answer: you need to know enough to show one sample. The sample is more persuasive than more study. Learn in response to a client's question, not in advance. - "I hate sales." Answer: the video's point is that outbound is a numbers game with a repeatable script (Module 3). Reduce the fear by setting a small daily number and tracking it. - Risk: an unrealistic timeline. The video says a first client can come within a month; that is not a guarantee. Plan for several months of outreach with your day job still paying your bills.

Key terms for quick reference: LLM, tutorial trap, outbound, pipeline.

Chapter 1.2: The knowledge gap: selling "new" versus "better or faster"

Video: 04:13–06:25

1. Core objective. Choose a service where businesses do not yet know how to buy, and position yourself as the guide, rather than competing on price with an established supplier.

2. Key concepts and mental models - Better or faster is a hard sell. The video argues that businesses rarely take meetings for an improvement on something they already buy. [Opinion] - New is an opening. A capability that was not possible before, or that the buyer does not understand, creates a knowledge gap. The seller who explains it is valued as an expert. [Opinion] - High demand, low supply. The video's selection rule: look for services many businesses want and few providers offer. [Opinion] - The arbitrage analogy. The video compares AI to early crypto consulting, when young people who had learned the field were hired by large companies that did not understand it. The analogy is about the knowledge gap, not a promise of similar results. [Video]

Key terms in this chapter

Term Definition
Arbitrage Profiting from a gap between what one party knows or can do and what another party needs.
Blue ocean A market with little competition. The video uses the term for AI services. Verify the competition before you rely on it.
Demand How many buyers want a service and would pay for it.
Supply How many providers already offer it.
Perceived expert Someone a buyer sees as knowing more than them, even if they are still learning.
Positioning The specific problem and customer you say you solve.

3. Step-by-step SOP checklist - [ ] List five services you could sell with AI. For each, write who buys it, what they pay now, and what the result is. - [ ] Score each 1 to 5 on demand (are buyers searching for it or hiring for it?) and on supply (search the service on LinkedIn and Upwork: how many providers are there?). - [ ] Keep the service with the highest demand-to-supply estimate. Mark the score as an estimate; you are judging from search results, not market data. - [ ] Write a one-sentence positioning: "I help [type of business] get [result] using AI, in [timeframe]."

4. Objections and risk mitigation - "Is this market real, or is it hype?" Answer: check it. Look for paid job posts, competitors' pricing pages, and reviews of agencies offering the same service. The video gives no market size data. [Check] - "Won't the market fill up?" Answer: yes, supply will grow. Build a repeatable process and a niche early, so you have an advantage when it does. - Risk: choosing a service because a video says it is hot. Validate with five real conversations before you commit.

Key terms for quick reference: arbitrage, blue ocean, demand, supply, positioning.

The video says many teams do not know how to use AI to produce good ads at scale. [Video] - Offer two: AEO (answer engine optimisation): helping a company appear in answers from AI assistants such as ChatGPT and Claude. The video calls this "the new Google search". You deliver a result that helps the business make money, and AI does most of the work (the video says 70 to 90%). [Video] - Example price in the video: an offer of about $2,000 to $3,000 a month for AI Meta ads. [Video] Treat that as a starting point to test, not a market price.

Module 2: The Claude and Hostinger Credibility Stack

Chapter 2.1: Headless site deployment with a Claude connector and Hostinger

Video: 10:46–13:04

1. Core objective. Connect your hosting account to Claude and deploy a live landing page from a single instruction, so that you can send a working link to a prospect.

2. Key concepts and mental models - Why a website at all? When a prospect hears you, they look you up. A live page with samples and a booking link is the credibility you do not yet have. [Video] - Hosting. The service that stores your website files and serves them on the internet. - Domain. The address people type (for example, yourname.com). Hosting and domain are separate, although providers often sell both. - Connector / MCP. The video connects Claude to Hostinger through a connector built on the Model Context Protocol (MCP): a standard that lets an AI assistant use an external service's actions. The video shows the connector being added in Claude's settings. [Video, Check the current UI] - Headless deployment. Here it means: the assistant builds and publishes the site, and you do not manually use the hosting provider's dashboard.

Key terms in this chapter

Term Definition
MCP (Model Context Protocol) An open standard that connects AI assistants to tools and services through a defined interface.
Connector A link that gives an AI assistant permission to use a specific service, such as a hosting account.
Hosting Storing a website's files on a server so that it can be viewed on the internet.
Domain The human-readable web address of a site.
Deployment Publishing a finished website to a live server.

3. Step-by-step SOP checklist (steps as described in the video; check the current interface) - [ ] Open Claude, go to Settings, then Connectors. - [ ] Search for Hostinger and connect your Hostinger account. Open the link on a phone and on a laptop. - [ ] Check the page yourself: every claim, every number, every link. Remove anything you cannot prove.

[Check] - "The page says things that are not true." The assistant writes plausible copy, and it can invent statistics. Check every number and remove any you cannot source. - "Hosting costs add up." Start with the smallest plan that works, and check current prices on the provider's site before you buy. The video's discount offers and sponsor terms are not reproduced here. [Check] - Risk: publishing a site that uses another company's logo or claims as if it were theirs. See Chapter 2.3.

Chapter 2.2: The credibility stack: offer, samples and booking link

Video: 08:17–10:31 (the four steps) and 13:04–14:22 (the booking flow and samples)

1. Core objective. Assemble the four pieces a prospect sees before a call: a clear offer, samples of work, a way to book a call, and a plain statement of your experience.

2. Key concepts and mental models - The four-step path in the video: (1) an offer; (2) outreach by a direct message; (3) the prospect books a call on your website; (4) if they like what they see, you have the call and close. [Video] - Credibility without clients. The video's argument: you cannot show a testimonial yet, but you can show work. Samples, a process and an honest statement of your experience do the job. [Opinion] - Honesty about being new. The video suggests saying you are breaking into the field and that the tools are new, rather than pretending to long experience. [Video] - Booking link. A calendar link on your site lets a prospect who is interested act immediately, which the video says reduces friction. [Video]

Key terms in this chapter

Term Definition
Social proof Evidence that others trust you: testimonials, logos, case studies, results.
Sample A piece of work you have made to show what you can do, clearly marked as a sample or concept.
Booking link A calendar link that lets a prospect choose a time for a call without emailing back and forth.
Scheduling tool Software that manages calendar availability and bookings.
Discovery call A first conversation to understand the prospect's problem (Module 4).

3. Step-by-step SOP checklist - [ ] Write the offer on one page: problem, deliverable, timeframe, price range, and what you need from the client to start. - [ ] Prepare three samples. Label each "concept sample, not made for this client" unless you have permission to use the brand. - [ ] Write a three-line bio: what you do, what tools you use, and an honest sentence about your experience. - [ ] Add one booking link from a calendar tool of your choice. Test it by booking yourself. - [ ] Add a contact method that is not only the booking link.

4. Objections and risk mitigation - "Nobody will take a meeting with someone with no experience." The video's answer is that a clear, specific sample is more persuasive than a long CV. Test this with your first ten prospects and record the reply rate. - "Samples look like AI slop." Put your sample next to the reference you used and explain the choices (Chapter 2.3). - Risk: a bio that claims results you do not have. Use only facts you can show.

Key terms for quick reference: social proof, sample, booking link, scheduling tool, discovery call.

Chapter 2.3: Building bespoke client demonstration landing pages

Video: 14:22–21:53

1. Core objective. Build a demonstration page for one named prospect, using a reference that performs well, and explain every design and copy choice, so that the prospect sees your method, not only the output.

2. Key concepts and mental models - Reference-led creation. The video's method: find high-performing ads or pages (through a public library), analyse what makes them work, write the pattern down, and apply it to the prospect's brand. [Video] - Winning-ad signals (Meta). The video says there are eight signals that show a Meta ad is good, and names four: how many days the ad has been running, how many variations (duplicates) exist, impressions, and the countries it runs in. It also suggests that an ad working in several languages and cultures is a stronger signal. The list in the video is incomplete. The other signals must be confirmed by you from the data. [Video, Check] - Reading an ad library. Meta publishes an Ad Library of current ads from advertisers, free to view. Use it to see how long an ad has run and how many variants a brand tests. [Check] the current scope of the library and its terms. - Remix, do not copy. The video recommends changing the reference (brand, product, copy) and adding your own angle, rather than copying a competitor's creative. [Opinion] - Tokens and context. When you ask an AI assistant to analyse ads, it works within a context window: the amount of text it can consider in one conversation. Long inputs use more tokens, which are the small pieces of text (roughly words or word parts) an AI model reads and writes. Paste only the ads and data you need. [General]

Key terms in this chapter

Term Definition
Ad library A public database of advertisements run by platforms such as Meta.
Impressions The number of times an ad has been displayed.
Variation A version of an ad with changed copy, image or format, tested alongside others.
Context window The maximum amount of text an AI model can consider at once.
Token A piece of text an AI model reads or writes; the unit of most AI usage limits and prices.
Prompt The instructions you give an AI assistant.
Skill (Claude) A saved set of instructions that Claude can reuse on new inputs. The video suggests saving the ad analysis as one. [Video, Check the current feature]
Mock-up A realistic draft of a design, shown as an idea rather than a finished product.
Trademark A registered brand name, logo or symbol that others may not use without permission.

3. Step-by-step SOP checklist - [ ] Choose three brands in the prospect's category. Use a public list or a search, and note your source. - [ ] Open each brand's entry in the ad library. Record for each ad: start date, variants, countries and languages, and whether it is still running. - [ ] Write the pattern: hook type, proof points (numbers, testimonials), format, product shot style, call to action. Mark which points come from the data and which are your opinion. - [ ] Ask your assistant to turn the pattern into a written guideline. Remove any claim you cannot support from the data. - [ ] Create nine concept ads for the prospect: three per brand in the prospect's style, in a simple HTML page, each labelled "concept". - [ ] Put one concept next to one reference ad and write three sentences on the difference. Keep them honest. - [ ] Publish the demonstration page with a clear title (for example, "Concept ads for [Brand]: not affiliated, not commissioned"). Do not use the brand's logo or product photos without permission.

4. Objections and risk mitigation - "Is it allowed to make ads for a real brand and send them?" Unsolicited concept work is common, but it must not imply that the brand endorses you or approved the work, and it must not use protected brand assets without permission. Label everything as concept work. [Check local law and the brand's policies.] - "The output looks generic." Make it specific to the prospect's audience and product, and explain the reasoning. The video's argument is that the reasoning is what buyers pay for. - "Claude might produce a claim that is false." Every number and claim must be checked against a source before it goes on the page. - Risk: the context problem. A long conversation with many brands can mix up details (a claim from one brand appears on another's page). Start a fresh conversation for each prospect, and check each page against its own brand's data. - Risk: copying. If the concept ad repeats a competitor's copy or image, you have copied it. Remix the pattern, not the content.

Key terms for quick reference: ad library, impressions, variation, context window, token, prompt, skill, mock-up, trademark.

Module 3: Signal-Based Prospecting and the DIQ Outbound Funnel

Chapter 3.1: The 800-connection monthly math: funnel benchmarks and conversion ratios

Video: 22:17–24:06

1. Core objective. Build a funnel calculator for your own outreach, using the video's ratios as a starting point, and set a daily number you can keep up with.

2. Key concepts and mental models - A funnel is a sequence of rates. Each step loses people. Your result equals the number at the top times each rate. - The video's funnel (LinkedIn): - About 800 connection requests a month. - Conservative acceptance rate: 28%. The video says about 40% is possible with an optimised profile. - About 225 accepted connections. - Message each one, with three follow-ups; about 10% reply, so about 22 replies. - About 3 to 5 calls booked from the replies. - Close about 25% of calls, so about 1 client. [Video, Estimate] - Inconsistency to note. The video says you are "allowed to do 800 connections" a week, and later works out 200 a week times four, which is 800 a month. The second calculation is the one the funnel uses. Check the platform's current weekly and monthly invitation limits before you plan. [Check] - Automation risk. The video does not say whether to automate these messages. LinkedIn's user agreement restricts automated activity and can lead to account restrictions. This course recommends manual outreach or approved tools only. [Check LinkedIn's current terms.] - The numbers game. The video's point: even a modest funnel produces one client a month when it runs every month. [Opinion]

Key terms in this chapter

Term Definition
Funnel A sequence of steps from first contact to paying client, with a rate at each step.
Conversion rate The share of people who move from one step to the next.
Acceptance rate The share of connection requests that are accepted.
Reply rate The share of messages that receive a reply.
Follow-up A second or third message sent when there is no reply.
Book-call rate The share of replies that lead to a booked call.
Close rate The share of calls that become a paying client.
Automation (outreach) Software that sends messages on your behalf. Risky under most platform terms unless the platform approves it.

3. Step-by-step SOP checklist - [ ] Write your own funnel: requests sent, acceptance rate, messages sent, reply rate, calls booked, close rate. Use the video's numbers only until you have your own data. - [ ] Compute the expected clients per month: requests × acceptance × reply × call × close. Check it: 800 × 0.28 × 0.10 × 0.15 (3–5 calls out of 22 replies is about 15%) × 0.25 ≈ 0.8 clients. [Estimate] - [ ] Set a daily number you can keep for 30 days. The video's example is 50 requests a day. [Video] - [ ] Track your numbers weekly. Replace the video's rates with yours after 30 days. - [ ] Before you start, check your platform's current limits and terms. [Check]

4. Objections and risk mitigation - "Outreach feels spammy." Answer: keep messages specific, relevant and short, and stop when someone says no. Good targeting (Chapter 3.2) reduces spam. - "Outreach fatigue." Answer: rotate between LinkedIn, email and referrals, and take a break in weeks with low results. - "What if my acceptance rate is low?" Answer: improve your profile first. The video says a strong profile raises acceptance. [Video] - Risk: sending mass messages that break the platform's rules, which can lose your account. Use manual outreach.

Key terms for quick reference: funnel, conversion rate, acceptance rate, reply rate, follow-up, close rate, automation.

Chapter 3.2: Crafting trigger-based lists: open job requisitions and funding events

Video: 26:14–28:22

1. Core objective. Build a list of 10 prospects who have a signal showing a need, and write down the signal next to each name.

2. Key concepts and mental models - List quality is most of the result. The video says the list is about 70% of the success, more than the message. [Video, Opinion] - Signals. A signal is public information that makes a prospect more likely to need your service right now. Examples from the video: a company raised funding, a company is hiring aggressively, a company has been trying to fill a role for weeks, or a company is hiring for the exact service you sell. [Video] - Job requisition. An open job posting. A role that has been open for a long time is a stronger signal, because the company has a problem it has not solved. [General] - Funding event. A public announcement that a company has raised money. Funding can mean budget for new services. [General; the funding may not be spent on your service] - Using the assistant for lists. The video asks Claude to find companies that match a set of signals. The assistant can help you organise the search, but you must check every company and every fact yourself, because assistants can make things up. [Check]

Key terms in this chapter

Term Definition
Signal Public evidence that a prospect has a need or a budget.
Trigger A specific event (a funding round, a new hire, a job post) that makes outreach timely.
Job requisition An open role that a company is recruiting for.
ICP (ideal customer profile) A description of the companies that gain most from your service: size, sector, stage and role.
Lead list A spreadsheet of prospects with names, roles, company, signal and status.
Enrichment Adding verified details (role, company size, contact route) to a prospect record.
VC funding Investment from venture capital firms, usually reported in press releases or databases.

3. Step-by-step SOP checklist - [ ] Write your ICP in one paragraph: company size, sector, country, the role you contact, and the problem. - [ ] Choose three signals from the video's examples (funding, open role, hiring for the service). - [ ] Ask your assistant to propose ten companies that match the ICP and the signals. Give it the criteria, not a conclusion. - [ ] For each company, open the job post or funding announcement and confirm it yourself. Delete any company you cannot verify. - [ ] In the spreadsheet, record: company, contact role, signal, source link, date checked. - [ ] Write one sentence per company on the problem the signal suggests (for example, "role open for 45 days, so the team may be short of creative capacity").

4. Objections and risk mitigation - "I'm making up a problem for them." Answer: the signal is a question, not a claim. Your message asks, it does not assert. Only pitch when the signal is real. - "The list is out of date by the time I contact them." Answer: check the date of each signal and drop anything older than your outreach window. - "Am I allowed to collect this data?" Use public sources, and do not collect personal data beyond what you need for business contact. Follow the privacy rules in your country (for example, India's DPDP Act and the EU's GDPR where they apply). [Check] - Risk: a list that targets people who cannot buy (for example, a junior employee who cannot approve a budget). Aim for decision-makers or the team lead.

Key terms for quick reference: signal, trigger, job requisition, ICP, lead list, enrichment, VC funding.

Chapter 3.3: The Data-Insight-Question (DIQ) outreach script and Loom hooks

Video: 24:45–26:14

1. Core objective. Write a first message with the DIQ structure (data, insight, question) and a Loom video hook, and send it only after the prospect has accepted your connection.

2. Key concepts and mental models - DIQ. The video's formula: (1) lead with a data point you found; (2) give an insight or opinion about it; (3) ask a question. Do not ask for the call and do not ask for the sale. [Video] - Why a question. A question invites a reply and reveals the prospect's problem. A pitch closes the conversation. [Opinion] - Connect without a note. The video's method: send the connection request with no note, and send the first message after it is accepted. [Video] - Loom hook. A short recorded video that shows a prospect something specific, such as a breakdown of a competitor's ads. Offer it, do not send it unasked. The video suggests asking first ("want me to send it over?"), so you do not spend time on a video nobody wants. [Video] - Positioning as an agency, not an employee. The video's framing: you offer to do the work, not to take the job. [Video]

3. Step-by-step SOP checklist - [ ] For each prospect, find one data point from the signal (for example, "the creative strategist role has been open for 45 days"). - [ ] Write the insight in one sentence (for example, "finding good creative talent is hard right now"). - [ ] Write the question. Keep it open: "Is that something you're trying to solve this quarter?" - [ ] Draft the message: thanks or context, data, insight, question. Keep it under 80 words. - [ ] Only if the prospect replies with interest, offer a short Loom video: "Want me to send over a quick breakdown of how I'd approach your ads?" - [ ] Send follow-ups at intervals you set in advance (the video's example is three follow-ups in total). Stop after the last one.

Sample DIQ first message (for a practice prospect, edit before use):

Hi [Name], thanks for connecting. I noticed the creative strategist role at [Company] has been open for about six weeks. Finding people with good creative judgement for ads at pace is hard right now. Is filling that role a priority this quarter, or are you looking at other ways to add capacity?

4. Objections and risk mitigation - "That sounds like a cold pitch with a question on the end." Answer: that is the reason for the data point. The message must show you looked, and the question must be real. - "What if the data point is wrong?" Answer: check it before sending. A wrong data point ends the conversation. - "Follow-ups feel annoying." Answer: three short follow-ups with new value each time is the video's method. Stop when asked. - Risk: sending a Loom video or a deliverable that the prospect did not ask for, which reads as spam.

Module 4: Consultative Discovery and High-Ticket Closing

Chapter 4.1: The agency versus full-time hire cost arbitrage frame

Video: 28:22–29:09

1. Core objective. Explain to a prospect why a monthly agency fee is cheaper and less risky than a full-time hire, using your own numbers.

2. Key concepts and mental models - The comparison in the video. An agency or freelancer at about $3,000 a month, against a full-time hire costing about $6,000 to $11,000 a month. [Video] The $11,000 upper figure is taken from the course brief. Replace both with the prospect's real salary data and the cost of recruiting, because salaries vary by country and role. [Check] - Three hidden costs of a hire (from the video): months of searching (opportunity cost), the time to train someone in AI, and the risk that the output is poor while you pay. [Video] - Arbitrage. The gap between the cost of a result and what the buyer pays now. Your job is to show the buyer that gap clearly. - Unit economics. The cost of delivering one unit of work, compared with the price. For a retainer, check that your time per client, tool costs and any contractor costs leave a margin you are happy with. [General] - Retainer. A fixed monthly fee. [Video, Check]

Key terms in this chapter

Term Definition
Cost arbitrage Paying less for the same result by choosing a cheaper way to get it.
Opportunity cost The value of what you give up when you choose one option, such as months spent recruiting.
Retainer A recurring monthly fee for ongoing services.
Unit economics The revenue and cost of delivering one unit of your service, and the margin between them.
Margin The share of revenue left after costs.
Total cost of employment Salary plus taxes, benefits, equipment and recruiting costs for an employee.

3. Step-by-step SOP checklist - [ ] Get the prospect's salary range for the role they would hire, from a job post or a salary survey for their location. [Check] - [ ] Add a realistic total employment cost (salary plus benefits and recruiting). Mark the figure as an estimate. - [ ] Write your retainer and deliverables on one page. Compare: monthly fee, deliverables, time to start, what happens if the work is poor. - [ ] Calculate your margin: monthly fee minus your hours at a stated rate minus tool costs. Make sure the margin works. - [ ] Prepare one sentence for the call: "For about the cost of a [role], you get [deliverables] from day one, with a team that already uses AI."

4. Objections and risk mitigation - "We can hire a junior person for less." Answer: then compare the total cost, including the training and the output quality. Ask what the junior would produce in a month. - "Three thousand is too much for AI." Answer: the buyer pays for results and judgement, not for the tool. Show the sample and the process. - "What happens if you stop?" Answer: state your notice period and a handover process. Buyers worry about dependency. - Risk: under-pricing. A low fee attracts clients who expect too much. Price to your margin.

Key terms for quick reference: cost arbitrage, opportunity cost, retainer, unit economics, margin, total cost of employment.

Chapter 4.2: Running frictionless problem-led discovery calls

Video: 29:09–30:00

1. Core objective. Run a 20-minute discovery call that finds the prospect's problems in their own words, then proposes a solution only when the prospect asks for one.

2. Key concepts and mental models - Problem first. The video's call structure: ask what their problems are (your list is built around them), ask permission to share what you do, then give a solution matched to the problems they named. [Video] - Permission-based pitching. Asking "do you mind if I share what I do?" changes the call from a pitch into a conversation. [Video] - The offer in plain words. For example: "You send us your brand, we produce the ads, you test them, and we repeat every week." Keep it short. [Video] - Sales is a conversation. The video's view: you are showing the prospect that you can do the work. The call is not a performance. [Opinion] - Practice. The video recommends live role-play. You can practise with a colleague or in a mirror before a real call. [Opinion]

Key terms in this chapter

Term Definition
Discovery call A first call to understand the prospect's problem, budget and timing.
Qualifying Checking whether the prospect has the problem, the budget and the authority to act.
Objection A reason the prospect gives for not buying (price, timing, trust).
Next step The specific action agreed at the end of a call, such as a proposal or a sample review.
Problem statement One sentence that names the prospect's problem in their own words.
Authority Whether the person you are speaking with can approve the purchase.

3. Step-by-step SOP checklist - [ ] Before the call: review the prospect's signal and your sample. Prepare three questions about their problem. - [ ] Open (1 minute): thank them, state the purpose ("I'd like to understand your ads and what's getting in the way"). - [ ] Problem (8 minutes): ask about current process, results, what has been tried, and what a good result would look like. - [ ] Permission (1 minute): "Do you mind if I share how I'd approach this?" - [ ] Offer (5 minutes): match each problem they named to one part of your deliverable. Show the sample that fits. - [ ] Close (3 minutes): ask about budget, decision-maker and timing. Agree a next step with a date. - [ ] After the call (within 24 hours): send a short summary of what you heard and the next step.

4. Objections and risk mitigation - "Your price is too high." Answer: ask what they compare it with. Return to the cost frame (Chapter 4.1) and offer a smaller first deliverable if it fits. - "We need to think about it." Answer: ask what specifically they need to think about, and agree a date to follow up. - "Who else are you working with?" Answer: state your current clients honestly. If you have none, say so and offer your sample process. - "Can you prove this works?" Answer: show the sample, explain the reasoning, and offer a paid pilot with a clear measure. - Risk: talking more than listening. Aim for the prospect to speak for most of the call.

Key terms for quick reference: discovery call, qualifying, objection, next step, problem statement, authority.

Capstone Sprint: Prospect Landing Page and 50-Contact Campaign

Duration: one weekend to build, then 30 days of outreach.

Deliverables 1. An ICP and a list of 10 prospects, each with a verified signal and source link. 2. One sample landing page for one prospect, built with an AI assistant and checked line by line. Labelled "concept sample". 3. Three concept ads for the prospect, each with a short explanation of the reasoning. 4. A 50-contact outreach campaign: 50 connection requests, DIQ messages to those who accept, and up to three follow-ups. 5. A funnel report at day 30: requests sent, acceptance, replies, calls booked, and what you would change.

Evaluation rubric

Criterion What good looks like Weight
Signal quality Every prospect has a verified, dated signal 20%
Sample quality Specific, accurate, labelled, with reasoning 25%
Outreach discipline Manual or approved methods, correct DIQ format, follow-up rules kept 20%
Funnel report Honest numbers, clear next changes 20%
Compliance No brand misuse, no unverified claims, platform terms respected 15%

Pitfalls to avoid: spending the weekend on the tool and not the outreach; sending messages without checking signals; making a sample that copies a competitor; and reporting results you did not measure.
