Business Fundamentals :: A business earns revenue and pays costs to make profit. Cash flow timing matters as much as profit, because a growing business can still run out of cash. Product decisions change revenue, cost and cash, so PMs need this vocabulary.
Business Model Canvas :: The canvas has nine blocks: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners and cost structure. It helps check that the parts of a business fit together.
Revenue Models :: Common models include subscription, transaction fee, advertising, licensing, usage-based pricing, marketplace commission and services. Each model shapes what the product should optimise for.
Subscription Business Models :: Customers pay a recurring fee for continued access. Revenue is predictable, but retention becomes the main lever. Improving onboarding and value delivery matters as much as acquisition.
Freemium Models :: A free tier drives adoption and conversion to paid plans. It works when the cost of serving free users is low and the upgrade trigger is clear, such as a limit that users hit when they are already getting value.
Usage-Based Pricing :: Customers pay according to consumption, such as API calls or messages sent. The price matches value, but revenue is less predictable, so forecasting and customer budgets need extra attention.
Product Pricing Strategy :: Pricing uses cost, competitor prices and the value to the customer. Packaging into tiers fits different segments. Test price changes on new customers first, because changing prices for existing customers creates churn risk.
Monetisation Strategies :: These are the mechanisms by which a product earns money and when in the journey they appear. A paywall shown before any value is delivered often reduces activation.
Financial Modelling :: A financial model is a spreadsheet of revenue, costs and cash under stated assumptions. Build best, base and worst scenarios and state which assumptions matter most, so the model supports decisions rather than hiding risk.
Unit Economics :: Unit economics measures profit or loss per customer or transaction after variable costs. Key measures are CAC, LTV and payback period. Healthy unit economics mean each customer pays back acquisition cost within an acceptable time.
ROI and ROE :: ROI is the gain from an investment divided by its cost. ROE is net income divided by shareholder equity. For product work, ROI is the more common tool to compare investment options.
Cost-Benefit Analysis :: This compares total expected costs and benefits in money terms, including time saved and risk reduced. Quantify benefits conservatively and show the assumptions, so the comparison is credible.
Break-Even Analysis :: Break-even is the volume at which revenue equals total cost. It equals fixed costs divided by contribution margin per unit. It shows how many customers a new product must win to stop losing money.
Profitability Analysis :: Profitability analysis looks at margins by product, segment or channel to see where money is made or lost. A profitable product line can hide an unprofitable segment that drives support costs.
Business Case Development :: A business case argues for an investment: problem, options, costs, benefits, risks and a recommendation. Lead with the decision requested. Include the cost of doing nothing.
Budget Planning :: Budget planning allocates funds to teams and initiatives, tracks spending against plan and forecasts the rest of the year. Keep a reserve for unexpected work, such as an urgent compliance change.
Go-to-Market Strategy :: The GTM strategy defines who the product is for, the message, the price, the channels and how success will be measured after launch. It connects product decisions with sales and marketing.
Product Launch Strategy :: A launch plan covers readiness checks, a beta with selected users, announcement, sales enablement, support preparation and post-launch monitoring. The launch date should follow the readiness checklist, not the other way round.
Product Adoption :: Adoption moves customers from awareness to trial to regular use. Onboarding, first success and reminders at the right moments drive adoption more than announcements.
Customer Acquisition :: Acquisition channels include content, paid media, partners, sales and referrals. Compare channels by cost, quality of customers and payback. A channel that brings many customers who never activate is expensive.
Customer Retention Strategy :: Retention comes from onboarding, ongoing value, support, community and proactive outreach to users who show declining engagement. Retention improvements often pay back faster than new acquisition.
Product Growth Strategy :: Growth strategy identifies loops and levers across acquisition, activation, retention, revenue and referral. Choose the lever with the largest gap in the funnel and test changes against it.
Product-Led Sales :: Product usage signals identify accounts ready to buy. Sales then engages those accounts with context from the product. It blends self-serve adoption with human sales for larger deals.
Enterprise Product Commercialisation :: Selling to large organisations involves security reviews, procurement, pilots, integration and long sales cycles. Plan these steps into the product roadmap, such as single sign-on and audit logs, because they often decide the deal.
