Introduction to Product Management :: Product management connects customer problems, business goals and technical feasibility. A PM decides what to build and why, then makes sure it reaches customers. Example: a fintech app notices users abandon KYC; the PM frames the problem before any feature is discussed.
Evolution of Product Management :: The role grew from brand management at consumer goods firms to software product management in the 2000s, and now includes data-driven and AI-assisted practice. Knowing this history explains why modern PMs are expected to run experiments and understand technology.
Product Manager Roles and Responsibilities :: A PM owns the problem, the vision, the roadmap and the outcome metrics, but not the team. Influence comes from clarity: writing decisions down, sharing evidence and keeping engineering, design and sales aligned on one goal.
Product Manager vs Product Owner vs Project Manager vs Business Analyst :: The PM owns the product strategy and outcomes. The product owner manages the backlog inside a Scrum team. The project manager tracks schedule, budget and delivery. The business analyst details requirements and processes. Confusion between these roles causes most early friction in new teams.
Product Manager vs Product Analyst vs Product Operations :: The analyst answers questions with data and builds dashboards. Product operations builds the processes, tools and data pipelines that let teams work well. The PM turns those inputs into decisions about what to build next.
Types of Product Managers :: Core PMs ship features in one area, technical PMs own platforms and APIs, growth PMs optimise funnels, and AI PMs manage uncertainty from models. Pick the type that matches your background, then build the skills it needs.
Product Lifecycle Management :: Products move through introduction, growth, maturity and decline. Each stage needs different investment: acquisition early, retention and cost control in maturity, and a sunset plan at the end. Example: a legacy reporting tool may be kept alive only for a few enterprise clients.
Product Discovery vs Product Delivery :: Discovery answers whether a problem is worth solving, using interviews, prototypes and experiments. Delivery builds and ships the validated solution reliably. Teams that skip discovery build well-engineered features nobody uses.
Product Thinking and Customer-Centricity :: Product thinking starts from the user's job and pain, not from a solution a stakeholder wants. Ask what changes for the customer if you ship the feature. A useful test is whether you can state the problem without mentioning your product.
Product Vision, Mission and Objectives :: The mission explains why the company exists. The vision describes the future the product enables, for example a world where every small shop can accept digital payments. Objectives break that vision into measurable steps for the next quarter or year.
Product Strategy Fundamentals :: Strategy is a set of choices about which customers to serve, which problems to solve and how to win against alternatives. A good strategy states what you will not do. Without that, roadmaps become lists of requests.
Product-Market Fit :: Product-market fit means a clear group of customers depends on the product and would be disappointed if it disappeared. Signs include retention that flattens over time, organic referrals and growing willingness to pay. It is a milestone you test for, not a feeling.
Product-Led Growth :: In product-led growth the product itself drives acquisition and expansion through free tiers, self-serve onboarding and sharing features. Slack and Dropbox are common examples. It works best when value is visible within minutes of signing up.
B2B, B2C, B2B2C and SaaS Product Models :: B2B sells to organisations with long sales cycles and many stakeholders. B2C sells to consumers at scale with simpler decisions. B2B2C sells through a business to consumers, such as a bank offering a loan app through an employer. SaaS is subscription software delivered online, which shapes pricing and retention work.
Product Management Operating Models :: Teams organise around features, streams of value or platforms. Some companies embed PMs in each team, others centralise them. The right model depends on how much coordination the product needs and how fast the company changes direction.
Product Management Career Paths :: A common path runs associate PM, PM, senior PM, group PM, director and chief product officer. Specialist paths in AI, growth or platforms are also valid. Build depth in one area and breadth in the others.
