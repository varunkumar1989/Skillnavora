Design Thinking :: Design thinking is a loop of empathise, define, ideate, prototype and test. It helps teams explore a problem widely before choosing a solution. Example: a team interviews delivery riders before designing a new earnings screen, and tests two layouts with five riders.
Problem Identification and Validation :: A problem is worth solving when it is frequent, painful and valuable enough that people change behaviour or pay. Validate by checking how often users face it and what they already do about it. Workarounds are a strong signal of real need.
Customer Research Methodologies :: Interviews explain motivations, surveys measure patterns at scale, observation reveals real behaviour, and analytics shows what users actually do. Choose the method that answers the decision you must make, and combine two methods where possible.
Qualitative and Quantitative Research :: Qualitative research explains why people act as they do, through small interviews and open questions. Quantitative research measures how many people act a certain way. Use qualitative work to find the problem and quantitative work to size it.
Customer Interviews :: A good interview asks about past behaviour and specific stories, not hypothetical future use. Avoid pitching your idea. Ask "tell me about the last time you did this" and let the customer describe the context and workarounds.
Surveys and Feedback Collection :: Surveys work when questions are short, unbiased and tied to a decision. Asking "would you use this?" gives misleading results because people are polite. Ask about current behaviour, then follow up with interviews to understand the answers.
User Personas :: Personas are evidence-based descriptions of key user groups, including goals, context and frustrations. Build them from interviews and data, not from imagination. Use them to check each decision: which persona does this help, and which does it hurt?
Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) :: People hire products to make progress in a specific situation, for example "help me send money home quickly on payday". Focusing on the job reveals competitors you did not expect, such as a notebook, or a relative to call.
Customer Pain Points :: Pain points are specific frustrations in a workflow, such as re-entering data twice or waiting for approval. Rank them by how often they occur and how much they cost the customer. The highest-ranked pain is usually the best place to start.
Customer Journey Mapping :: A journey map lays out the stages a customer goes through, with actions, thoughts, emotions and touchpoints at each step. It shows where people drop off and where the experience breaks. Build it from real interview stories, then compare it with analytics.
User Journey Analysis :: This examines how users move through the product using event data, such as which paths lead to success and which lead to dead ends. Look for repeated detours, because they often reveal confusing labels or missing features.
Empathy Mapping :: An empathy map captures what a user says, thinks, does and feels. It aligns the team on a shared picture of the user before debating solutions. Keep it short and grounded in quotes from real research.
Voice of Customer (VoC) :: VoC combines feedback from support tickets, reviews, sales calls and surveys into themes. Tag each comment, count themes over time and share the top themes with the team every month so customer voice shapes priorities.
Market Research :: Market research covers the size of demand, trends, customer segments and competitor moves. Combine secondary sources such as industry reports with primary interviews. Be clear about which numbers are estimates and how you derived them.
Market Segmentation :: Segmentation divides a market into groups with different needs, behaviours or willingness to pay. A useful segment is large enough to matter, reachable through a channel and different enough to need its own product or message.
TAM, SAM and SOM :: TAM is the total demand for the category, SAM is the part your model and geography can serve, and SOM is the share you can realistically win in the near term. Build each from clear assumptions, such as users times price times frequency, and keep SOM conservative.
Competitor Analysis :: Compare competitors on target customer, price, features, distribution and weaknesses reported by users. Look for gaps nobody serves well. Competitor analysis should change at least one decision, otherwise it is only a list.
SWOT Analysis :: SWOT lists strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats. It is quick, but it often becomes a list of generic points. Make each item specific and link it to an action, so the analysis leads to a decision.
Porter's Five Forces :: Porter's model assesses industry attractiveness through rivalry, threat of new entrants, substitutes, buyer power and supplier power. It explains why some markets are profitable and others are crowded. Use it to judge whether a market is worth entering.
Value Proposition Canvas :: The canvas matches customer jobs, pains and gains against your products, pain relievers and gain creators. It shows whether your offer addresses what customers actually care about. Gaps in the canvas point to features you may not need.
Opportunity Assessment :: An opportunity assessment is a short brief covering the problem, audience, market size, existing alternatives, why now, success measures and risks. It lets leaders compare opportunities on the same basis before committing people.
Hypothesis Development and Validation :: Write each assumption as a testable statement with a metric and a threshold, for example "at least 30% of trial users will invite a teammate within 7 days". Then run the cheapest test that could prove it wrong, before building anything large.
