Agile Fundamentals :: Agile delivers work in short iterations, gathers feedback quickly and adapts plans to what is learned. It replaces long fixed plans with frequent checkpoints. The PM's job is to keep the backlog ordered by outcome value.
Agile Manifesto :: The manifesto values individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration and responding to change. The twelve principles add practical guidance such as frequent delivery and sustainable pace. Use the values as a test for practices, not as a rulebook.
Scrum Framework :: Scrum organises work into fixed-length sprints, with defined roles, events and artefacts. Each sprint produces an increment of working product. Teams adopt Scrum to create a predictable rhythm of planning, review and improvement.
Kanban Methodology :: Kanban visualises work on a board, limits work in progress and measures flow. It suits support and operations work with unpredictable arrivals. Limits prevent teams from starting too much and finishing nothing.
Scrum Roles and Responsibilities :: The product owner orders the backlog and maximises value. The Scrum master removes impediments and coaches the process. Developers build the increment. Each role has a distinct accountability, and blurring them is a common failure.
Product Owner Responsibilities :: The product owner manages the backlog, writes clear items, accepts work against criteria and represents customers to the team. A product owner who is absent from daily decisions cannot steer the team well.
Sprint Planning :: The team selects backlog items and agrees a sprint goal. Planning answers what can be delivered and how. A good sprint goal describes the outcome, so the team can adapt scope without losing purpose.
Daily Stand-ups :: The daily stand-up is a 15-minute check on progress toward the sprint goal and on blockers. It is not a status report to the manager. Use it to remove blockers quickly, for example by escalating a waiting API decision the same day.
Sprint Reviews :: The sprint review demonstrates the working increment to stakeholders and gathers feedback. It is the best place to show real software, not slides. Feedback here should change the backlog order.
Sprint Retrospectives :: The retrospective examines how the team worked and agrees small, specific improvements. Keep one or two actions per sprint and track whether they helped. Retrospectives fail when they produce long lists nobody follows.
Backlog Grooming :: Backlog grooming is the older term for refinement: keeping items clear, sized and ordered. Groom continuously rather than in one large session, so the team is never starting from scratch.
Story Points :: Story points measure relative size and complexity, not hours. A story of 5 points should feel about five times the effort of a 1-point story. Use points for forecasting, and do not compare teams by points.
Estimation Techniques :: Planning poker, t-shirt sizing and affinity estimation provide quick estimates. Reference-class estimates compare work to similar past items. Estimates are forecasts with error bars, so communicate ranges.
Velocity and Burndown Charts :: Velocity is the points completed per sprint, and burndown shows remaining work over time. They forecast delivery. Using them to judge individuals damages trust and distorts estimates.
Release Planning :: Release planning sequences sprints into releases with scope, dates, dependencies and risk buffers. Staged rollouts such as 5%, 25% and 100% of users, with rollback plans, belong here. A release is ready when its go/no-go criteria are met.
Dependency Management :: A dependency is work that relies on another team, system or vendor. List dependencies early, agree dates with those teams and track them in each sprint review. Hidden dependencies cause most missed release dates.
Risk and Issue Management :: A risk is a possible problem you can prevent or reduce. An issue is a problem that has already happened and needs action. Keep a short risk register with owners, likelihood, impact and the mitigation date.
Cross-Functional Team Collaboration :: Product, design, engineering, data, QA and go-to-market work toward one goal with shared context. Shared goals and regular demos prevent the handoff delays of separate silos.
Engineering Collaboration :: Involve engineers from discovery onward. They know the cost of options and can suggest simpler solutions. Treat trade-offs on scope and quality as joint decisions.
Product Delivery Lifecycle :: The lifecycle runs from idea to discovery, design, build, test, release, measurement and iteration. Measurement closes the loop: the results decide whether to invest more, change direction or stop.
Jira :: Jira tracks issues, sprints, boards and workflows. Keep the workflow simple, with few statuses, and use a clear naming and labelling convention. Reports are useful only if the data is kept current.
Confluence :: Confluence is a team wiki for specifications, decision records and knowledge. Link pages to Jira issues so context stays connected to the work. Archive old pages so people can find current ones.
Linear and Azure DevOps :: Linear is a fast, opinionated issue tracker popular with product teams. Azure DevOps combines boards, repositories and pipelines for engineering organisations. Choose the tool that fits how your engineering team already works.
