Subject 17: AI product discovery

Covered on the website   Week 5 · AI Product Management skills

1. Understand

Discovery is deciding what to build before building it. For AI, the first question is not 'can we use AI?' but 'does this problem need AI at all?'.

2. Why it matters

Many AI projects fail because the problem was never valuable, the data was not ready, or a simple rule would have worked better.

3. How it works

  • Find the problem: interview 8 to 10 users, observe their work, list pain points.
  • Use Jobs to be Done: 'When I ..., I want to ..., so that I ...'.
  • Test AI versus non-AI: could a rule, a form or a search solve it?
  • Check feasibility: data availability, accuracy needed, latency, privacy, cost.
  • Prioritise with impact, effort, risk and data readiness; compare build versus buy.
  • Write a business case: cost, benefit, ROI and payback.

4. Practical implementation

Work through the steps above and keep the result in your portfolio repository.

5. Code explained

  • Data readiness is often the blocker, not the model.
  • ROI for AI should include ongoing run cost and evaluation effort, not only build cost.

5b. Deep dive

Discovery methods: customer interviews (ask about the last time the problem happened), shadowing (watch the work), journey mapping, and a short survey to size the problem. Ask about behaviour, not about hypothetical features.

Jobs to be done format: 'When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].' It keeps the focus on the user's goal and shows which parts of the job AI can change.

Build vs buy vs rules: a rules engine or a search page may solve the problem in a week. AI earns its place when the input is free text, the judgement is fuzzy, and a 'good enough' answer is measurable.

Business case: benefit (hours saved x loaded cost x adoption rate + revenue effect), cost (build + run + evaluation + support over 12 to 24 months), risk (what if it is wrong), and the decision rule (go if payback is under the agreed period).

6. Testing

Present the problem, the users, the evidence and the decision to three people. Ask them to challenge it.

7. Troubleshooting

  • No clear user: go back to interviews.
  • Benefit is unmeasurable: pick one metric before building.

8. AI Product Manager perspective

  • This is the core AI PM job: choosing problems where AI is the right tool and writing down why.

9. Assignment

Write a discovery brief for one workflow in a company you know: problem, users, evidence, AI vs non-AI decision, feasibility, and a one-year ROI estimate with assumptions.

10. Interview preparation

Q: How do you decide whether a problem needs AI?
Check whether the task needs language or judgement that rules cannot handle, whether the data exists, and whether a simpler solution meets the need first.

Subject 19: AI product documentation

Covered on the website   Week 5 · AI Product Management skills

1. Understand

Product documents turn an idea into a shared plan: why we build it, for whom, what it must do, how we will know it works, and what could go wrong.

2. Why it matters

AI projects involve data, engineering, legal and business teams. A clear PRD prevents months of arguing about what 'done' means.

3. How it works

  • Write the problem statement and target users with two personas.
  • Describe the user journey and the AI-specific moments (where the AI answers, where a human approves).
  • Write functional and non-functional requirements (latency, privacy, availability, accuracy).
  • Write user stories and acceptance criteria.
  • Add architecture, data flow, evaluation strategy, risk register, success metrics and a roadmap.
  • Use the PRD template in Part G.

4. Practical implementation

Work through the steps above and keep the result in your portfolio repository.

5. Code explained

  • Acceptance criteria for AI must include quality thresholds, not only features.
  • The risk register must include hallucination, data leakage and cost overrun.

5b. Deep dive

PRD sections and what each must answer: problem (who, what pain, how often), users (two personas with goals and constraints), journey (where AI helps and where a person decides), requirements (testable), quality bar (numbers), data (sources, owners, permissions), risks (likelihood, impact, mitigation), and success metrics (baseline and target).

Writing testable requirements: 'The bot answers from approved sources and shows at least one citation for 95 percent of answers on the golden set' is testable; 'the bot is accurate' is not.

Decision log: record each important decision, the options, the trade-off and the date. Future you (and your reviewers) will ask why.

6. Testing

A developer and a lawyer should each be able to find their questions answered in the PRD without asking you.

7. Troubleshooting

  • Requirements too vague: rewrite each as a testable statement.
  • Missing non-functional requirements: add latency, privacy and uptime targets.

8. AI Product Manager perspective

  • The PRD is your main tool for alignment. Keep it short enough to read in 15 minutes.

9. Assignment

Write the PRD for your capstone using the Part G template.

10. Interview preparation

Q: What belongs in an AI PRD that a normal PRD does not need?
Quality thresholds and evaluation method, data sources and their permissions, human-in-the-loop points, and failure handling.

Subject 21: AI product management fundamentals

Covered on the website   Week 6 · AI Product Management skills

1. Understand

An AI PM decides what the product should do, how well it must do it, and what risks the business accepts. They work with engineers on how it is built.

2. Why it matters

Traditional PM skills still apply. AI adds uncertainty: outputs vary, quality must be measured, and data and governance decide what is possible.

3. How it works

  • Lifecycle: discover, prototype, evaluate, launch, monitor, improve.
  • Model selection and build versus buy, with cost and privacy in the decision.
  • Human-in-the-loop design: when people review, approve or override.
  • AI UX: show sources, show confidence, make correction easy, explain limits.
  • Responsible AI and governance: policies, approvals, audit logs, and who is accountable.
  • Adoption, retention and ROI after launch.

4. Practical implementation

Work through the steps above and keep the result in your portfolio repository.

5. Code explained

  • Traditional PM: features are deterministic. AI PM: quality is a distribution and must be measured.
  • The AI PM owns the evaluation bar.

5b. Deep dive

AI PM responsibilities: define the job the AI does, set the quality bar with the business, decide the human-in-the-loop design, agree the data plan with engineering and legal, and own the post-launch metrics.

Traditional PM vs AI PM: traditional features are deterministic and testable with pass or fail; AI features are probabilistic, so the PM defines acceptable error rates, monitors distributions, and plans fallbacks.

Governance basics: an approval process for new use cases, a risk classification (low, medium, high), documentation (model card, data sheet), and an incident process. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is a widely used reference.

6. Testing

Write a one-page AI product strategy for a workflow: target users, the AI role, the risks and the first release.

7. Troubleshooting

  • Stakeholders expect 100% accuracy: agree a realistic bar and a human fallback.
  • Adoption is low: test the workflow with users before building more features.

8. AI Product Manager perspective

  • Your job is to make trade-offs explicit: quality versus speed, cost versus coverage, automation versus control.

9. Assignment

Complete the AI PM strategy one-pager for your capstone.

10. Interview preparation

Q: How is AI product management different from traditional product management?
Outputs are probabilistic, so the PM must define and monitor quality thresholds, plan for failure cases, and manage data and governance risk.

Subject 22: AI product metrics

Partial on the website   Week 6 · AI Product Management skills

1. Understand

Metrics tell you if the product works. For AI you need four families: technical (quality of the model and retrieval), product (use and success), customer (satisfaction), and business (cost and value).

2. Why it matters

A model can score well on accuracy and still fail users if they never adopt it. Each family catches a different failure.

3. How it works

  • Technical: accuracy, hallucination rate, retrieval precision and recall, latency.
  • Product: activation, engagement, task completion, resolution rate, human escalation rate.
  • Customer: satisfaction, complaints, trust rating.
  • Business: cost per task, time saved, revenue or cost impact, ROI.
  • North Star: pick the single metric that best predicts value, for example 'tasks completed correctly per week'.

4. Practical implementation

Work through the steps above and keep the result in your portfolio repository.

5. Code explained

  • Choose a metric you can measure every week.
  • Pair quality with cost: a higher-quality model is only better if the extra cost is worth it.

5b. Deep dive

Metric definitions to write down: numerator, denominator, time window, data source and owner. For example, resolution rate = tickets resolved by the assistant without human handoff / all assistant conversations, measured weekly.

Guardrail metrics stop a good-looking number from hiding harm: escalation rate, complaint rate and the hallucination sample rate. Always show them next to the main metric.

Choosing a North Star: it should predict long-term value, be measurable weekly, and be influenced by the team. For a support bot, correct resolutions per week; for a research tool, verified reports shipped per week.

6. Testing

Define 12 metrics (three from each family) with a target and a data source for each.

7. Troubleshooting

  • Metric is not measurable: write how you would collect it.
  • Vanity metrics: if a number cannot change a decision, drop it.

8. AI Product Manager perspective

  • Name the decision each metric informs. If no decision changes, the metric is noise.

9. Assignment

Produce a metrics table for your capstone with North Star, 3 technical, 3 product, 2 customer and 2 business metrics.

10. Interview preparation

Q: What is a North Star metric for an AI support assistant?
Resolution rate with a safe escalation rate: the share of customer issues solved correctly without harm, which captures both value and quality.

Subject 23: AI system design

Partial on the website   Week 6 · AI Product Management skills

1. Understand

System design means drawing the parts of a product and how data moves between them, then explaining the trade-offs.

2. Why it matters

Interviewers and engineering leads use system design to test whether you understand constraints like latency, cost, security and failure.

3. How it works

  • Clarify: users, scale, accuracy, latency, privacy, budget.
  • Functional and non-functional requirements.
  • Architecture: ingestion, retrieval, model, tools, storage, evaluation, monitoring.
  • Data flow and security boundaries.
  • Trade-offs: name at least two alternatives and why you chose one.
  • Metrics and failure modes.

4. Practical implementation

Work through the steps above and keep the result in your portfolio repository.

5. Code explained

  • Always name the failure you are most worried about and how the design limits it.

5b. Deep dive

Interview approach: clarify (users, scale, risk, budget), propose a simple version, draw the architecture, walk one request through it, then discuss failures and trade-offs. Stop at about 45 minutes with a clear next step.

Common components for an enterprise assistant: identity and access control, document ingestion with permissions, retrieval, model gateway (logging, limits, provider switch), tool layer with approvals, evaluation service, and monitoring.

Trade-offs to name: cost vs quality, latency vs accuracy, managed vs self-hosted models (data control), and automation vs human review (risk).

6. Testing

Present the design of a RAG chatbot for a bank in 10 minutes; a peer asks five challenge questions.

7. Troubleshooting

  • Diagram too complex: start with a three-box view, then zoom in.
  • Trade-offs missing: for each box ask 'what is the alternative and why not?'

8. AI Product Manager perspective

  • System design is where the PM and engineer agree on what must be true for the product to work.

9. Assignment

Design 3 systems from Part D's list (RAG chatbot, research agent, support assistant) using the template in Part G.

10. Interview preparation

Q: Design a customer support AI assistant for a bank.
Clarify scope and risk; retrieve from approved policy and product documents; use tools with approval for account changes; log everything; measure resolution and escalation; add a human handoff.
