SUBJECT 01Product Management Foundations16 topics

What a product manager does, how the role differs from its neighbours, and how a product moves from idea to growth. This subject sets the vocabulary used in every later subject.

  1. Introduction to Product ManagementThe discipline of deciding what to build, for whom and why, then guiding it from idea to a product that delivers customer and business value.

    Product management connects customer problems, business goals and technical feasibility. A PM decides what to build and why, then makes sure it reaches customers. Example: a fintech app notices users abandon KYC; the PM frames the problem before any feature is discussed.

  2. Evolution of Product ManagementFrom brand and packaged-goods managers, through software and agile teams, to today's data-driven and AI-assisted product teams.

    The role grew from brand management at consumer goods firms to software product management in the 2000s, and now includes data-driven and AI-assisted practice. Knowing this history explains why modern PMs are expected to run experiments and understand technology.

  3. Product Manager Roles and ResponsibilitiesOwning the problem, vision, strategy, roadmap, priorities and outcomes, and aligning engineering, design, data, marketing and sales around them.

    A PM owns the problem, the vision, the roadmap and the outcome metrics, but not the team. Influence comes from clarity: writing decisions down, sharing evidence and keeping engineering, design and sales aligned on one goal.

  4. Product Manager vs Product Owner vs Project Manager vs Business AnalystPM owns the why and what; the product owner manages the backlog in Scrum; the project manager owns schedule and delivery; the analyst owns detailed requirements and process.

    The PM owns the product strategy and outcomes. The product owner manages the backlog inside a Scrum team. The project manager tracks schedule, budget and delivery. The business analyst details requirements and processes. Confusion between these roles causes most early friction in new teams.

  5. Product Manager vs Product Analyst vs Product OperationsThe analyst answers questions with data; product operations builds the processes, tooling and insights that let PMs work faster; the PM decides what to do.

    The analyst answers questions with data and builds dashboards. Product operations builds the processes, tools and data pipelines that let teams work well. The PM turns those inputs into decisions about what to build next.

  6. Types of Product ManagersCore, technical, growth, platform, data, AI, B2B, B2C and enterprise PMs, each weighting skills differently.

    Core PMs ship features in one area, technical PMs own platforms and APIs, growth PMs optimise funnels, and AI PMs manage uncertainty from models. Pick the type that matches your background, then build the skills it needs.

  7. Product Lifecycle ManagementManaging a product through introduction, growth, maturity and decline, adjusting strategy, investment and metrics at each stage.

    Products move through introduction, growth, maturity and decline. Each stage needs different investment: acquisition early, retention and cost control in maturity, and a sunset plan at the end. Example: a legacy reporting tool may be kept alive only for a few enterprise clients.

  8. Product Discovery vs Product DeliveryDiscovery decides what is worth building through research and experiments; delivery builds and ships it. Strong teams run both continuously.

    Discovery answers whether a problem is worth solving, using interviews, prototypes and experiments. Delivery builds and ships the validated solution reliably. Teams that skip discovery build well-engineered features nobody uses.

  9. Product Thinking and Customer-CentricityStarting from the customer's problem and the outcome they want, then working back to features, instead of starting from solutions.

    Product thinking starts from the user's job and pain, not from a solution a stakeholder wants. Ask what changes for the customer if you ship the feature. A useful test is whether you can state the problem without mentioning your product.

  10. Product Vision, Mission and ObjectivesMission says why the company exists, vision describes the future state the product enables, and objectives set the measurable steps toward it.

    The mission explains why the company exists. The vision describes the future the product enables, for example a world where every small shop can accept digital payments. Objectives break that vision into measurable steps for the next quarter or year.

  11. Product Strategy FundamentalsA coherent set of choices on where to play and how to win, backed by evidence about customers, market and capabilities.

    Strategy is a set of choices about which customers to serve, which problems to solve and how to win against alternatives. A good strategy states what you will not do. Without that, roadmaps become lists of requests.

  12. Product-Market FitThe point at which a product satisfies strong market demand, seen in retention, organic growth and customers who would be very disappointed to lose it.

    Product-market fit means a clear group of customers depends on the product and would be disappointed if it disappeared. Signs include retention that flattens over time, organic referrals and growing willingness to pay. It is a milestone you test for, not a feeling.

  13. Product-Led GrowthA model where the product itself drives acquisition, conversion and expansion, through free tiers, trials, self-serve onboarding and viral loops.

    In product-led growth the product itself drives acquisition and expansion through free tiers, self-serve onboarding and sharing features. Slack and Dropbox are common examples. It works best when value is visible within minutes of signing up.

  14. B2B, B2C, B2B2C and SaaS Product ModelsWho buys and who uses differ by model: businesses, consumers, businesses serving consumers, and subscription software delivered online.

    B2B sells to organisations with long sales cycles and many stakeholders. B2C sells to consumers at scale with simpler decisions. B2B2C sells through a business to consumers, such as a bank offering a loan app through an employer. SaaS is subscription software delivered online, which shapes pricing and retention work.

  15. Product Management Operating ModelsHow product work is organised: feature teams, stream-aligned teams, platform teams, centralised or embedded PMs, and how they plan together.

    Teams organise around features, streams of value or platforms. Some companies embed PMs in each team, others centralise them. The right model depends on how much coordination the product needs and how fast the company changes direction.

  16. Product Management Career PathsAssociate PM to PM, senior, group, director and CPO, plus branches into specialist, founder and AI product roles.

    A common path runs associate PM, PM, senior PM, group PM, director and chief product officer. Specialist paths in AI, growth or platforms are also valid. Build depth in one area and breadth in the others.

SUBJECT 02Customer Discovery and Market Research22 topics

Methods to find real problems worth solving, understand customers deeply and size and test the opportunity before heavy investment.

  1. Design ThinkingA human-centred loop of empathise, define, ideate, prototype and test used to explore problems and solutions.

    Design thinking is a loop of empathise, define, ideate, prototype and test. It helps teams explore a problem widely before choosing a solution. Example: a team interviews delivery riders before designing a new earnings screen, and tests two layouts with five riders.

  2. Problem Identification and ValidationFinding a problem, then proving it is frequent, painful and valuable enough that people will change behaviour or pay.

    A problem is worth solving when it is frequent, painful and valuable enough that people change behaviour or pay. Validate by checking how often users face it and what they already do about it. Workarounds are a strong signal of real need.

  3. Customer Research MethodologiesInterviews, surveys, observation, diary studies, usability tests and data analysis, chosen to match the question.

    Interviews explain motivations, surveys measure patterns at scale, observation reveals real behaviour, and analytics shows what users actually do. Choose the method that answers the decision you must make, and combine two methods where possible.

  4. Qualitative and Quantitative ResearchQualitative research explains why people behave as they do; quantitative research measures how many and how much.

    Qualitative research explains why people act as they do, through small interviews and open questions. Quantitative research measures how many people act a certain way. Use qualitative work to find the problem and quantitative work to size it.

  5. Customer InterviewsOpen, non-leading conversations about past behaviour and real problems; ask for stories, not opinions about your idea.

    A good interview asks about past behaviour and specific stories, not hypothetical future use. Avoid pitching your idea. Ask "tell me about the last time you did this" and let the customer describe the context and workarounds.

  6. Surveys and Feedback CollectionStructured questions at scale; keep them short, unbiased and tied to a decision, and pair them with interviews.

    Surveys work when questions are short, unbiased and tied to a decision. Asking "would you use this?" gives misleading results because people are polite. Ask about current behaviour, then follow up with interviews to understand the answers.

  7. User PersonasEvidence-based archetypes of key user groups with goals, context and pain points, used to keep decisions grounded.

    Personas are evidence-based descriptions of key user groups, including goals, context and frustrations. Build them from interviews and data, not from imagination. Use them to check each decision: which persona does this help, and which does it hurt?

  8. Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)People hire products to make progress in a situation; the job, not demographics, explains what they choose.

    People hire products to make progress in a specific situation, for example "help me send money home quickly on payday". Focusing on the job reveals competitors you did not expect, such as a notebook, or a relative to call.

  9. Customer Pain PointsSpecific frustrations in a workflow, ranked by severity and frequency, that products can relieve.

    Pain points are specific frustrations in a workflow, such as re-entering data twice or waiting for approval. Rank them by how often they occur and how much they cost the customer. The highest-ranked pain is usually the best place to start.

  10. Customer Journey MappingA map of stages, actions, thoughts and emotions from awareness to loyalty, exposing gaps and moments that matter.

    A journey map lays out the stages a customer goes through, with actions, thoughts, emotions and touchpoints at each step. It shows where people drop off and where the experience breaks. Build it from real interview stories, then compare it with analytics.

  11. User Journey AnalysisExamining how users actually move through the product, using behaviour data to find drop-offs and detours.

    This examines how users move through the product using event data, such as which paths lead to success and which lead to dead ends. Look for repeated detours, because they often reveal confusing labels or missing features.

  12. Empathy MappingA one-page view of what a user says, thinks, does and feels to align the team on their perspective.

    An empathy map captures what a user says, thinks, does and feels. It aligns the team on a shared picture of the user before debating solutions. Keep it short and grounded in quotes from real research.

  13. Voice of Customer (VoC)Systematically collecting and synthesising customer feedback from support, reviews, sales calls and surveys.

    VoC combines feedback from support tickets, reviews, sales calls and surveys into themes. Tag each comment, count themes over time and share the top themes with the team every month so customer voice shapes priorities.

  14. Market ResearchGathering and analysing information about market size, trends, customers and competitors to guide decisions.

    Market research covers the size of demand, trends, customer segments and competitor moves. Combine secondary sources such as industry reports with primary interviews. Be clear about which numbers are estimates and how you derived them.

  15. Market SegmentationDividing a market into groups with shared needs or behaviours so you can target and position precisely.

    Segmentation divides a market into groups with different needs, behaviours or willingness to pay. A useful segment is large enough to matter, reachable through a channel and different enough to need its own product or message.

  16. TAM, SAM and SOMTotal addressable market, the serviceable part you can reach with your model, and the share you can realistically obtain.

    TAM is the total demand for the category, SAM is the part your model and geography can serve, and SOM is the share you can realistically win in the near term. Build each from clear assumptions, such as users times price times frequency, and keep SOM conservative.

  17. Competitor AnalysisComparing competitors' offers, pricing, positioning and strengths to find gaps and differentiation.

    Compare competitors on target customer, price, features, distribution and weaknesses reported by users. Look for gaps nobody serves well. Competitor analysis should change at least one decision, otherwise it is only a list.

  18. SWOT AnalysisStrengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats, a quick structured view of internal and external factors.

    SWOT lists strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats. It is quick, but it often becomes a list of generic points. Make each item specific and link it to an action, so the analysis leads to a decision.

  19. Porter's Five ForcesIndustry attractiveness from rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, buyer power and supplier power.

    Porter's model assesses industry attractiveness through rivalry, threat of new entrants, substitutes, buyer power and supplier power. It explains why some markets are profitable and others are crowded. Use it to judge whether a market is worth entering.

  20. Value Proposition CanvasMatches customer jobs, pains and gains to the products, pain relievers and gain creators you offer.

    The canvas matches customer jobs, pains and gains against your products, pain relievers and gain creators. It shows whether your offer addresses what customers actually care about. Gaps in the canvas point to features you may not need.

  21. Opportunity AssessmentA short brief that asks the key questions: problem, audience, size, alternatives, why now, success measures and risks.

    An opportunity assessment is a short brief covering the problem, audience, market size, existing alternatives, why now, success measures and risks. It lets leaders compare opportunities on the same basis before committing people.

  22. Hypothesis Development and ValidationTurn assumptions into testable statements with a metric and threshold, then run the cheapest test that could prove them wrong.

    Write each assumption as a testable statement with a metric and a threshold, for example "at least 30% of trial users will invite a teammate within 7 days". Then run the cheapest test that could prove it wrong, before building anything large.

SUBJECT 03Product Strategy and Roadmapping20 topics

Turning vision and research into focused strategy, an outcome-based roadmap, and defensible prioritisation and investment decisions.

  1. Product Vision and Strategic AlignmentConnecting the product vision to company strategy so teams share direction and priorities.

    Strategic alignment connects the product vision to company goals so teams share priorities. Without alignment, two teams can build excellent features that pull the company in opposite directions. Review the link between vision and goals every quarter.

  2. Strategic Product PlanningSetting goals, bets and sequencing over quarters and years, balancing growth, quality and capacity.

    Planning sets bets and sequencing across quarters and years, balancing growth, quality and team capacity. A plan should name the outcome each bet should move and what evidence would make you stop it.

  3. Product PositioningThe place your product holds in the customer's mind relative to alternatives: for whom, what category and why better.

    Positioning defines for whom the product is, which category it belongs to, and why it is better than the alternatives. A clear positioning statement lets sales, marketing and product describe the same thing.

  4. Product DifferentiationThe distinct, valued advantage that competitors cannot easily copy, such as data, workflow depth, brand or distribution.

    Differentiation is a valued advantage competitors cannot easily copy, such as proprietary data, deep workflow integration or a trusted brand. A feature anyone can copy is parity, not differentiation.

  5. Product Strategy FrameworksTools such as Playing to Win, Good Strategy (diagnosis, guiding policy, actions), Wardley maps and the three horizons model.

    Frameworks such as Playing to Win ask where to play and how to win. Good Strategy separates diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent actions. Wardley maps show how components evolve over time. Use one framework consistently rather than many at once.

  6. Product Roadmap DevelopmentA communication tool showing problems and outcomes planned over time, flexible on detail and honest about uncertainty.

    A roadmap communicates intent over time. Show problems and outcomes rather than dated promises for features. Mark what is committed, planned and exploratory, so stakeholders understand the uncertainty.

  7. Outcome-Based RoadmappingOrganising the roadmap around customer and business outcomes instead of a feature list with dates.

    An outcome roadmap groups work under the customer or business outcome it should change, such as reducing onboarding time by 30%. Teams then compete on ideas for that outcome rather than delivering a fixed list of features.

  8. OKRs and KPIsOKRs set ambitious objectives with measurable key results for a period; KPIs track ongoing health of the business or product.

    OKRs set ambitious objectives with measurable key results for a period. KPIs track ongoing health, such as uptime or monthly active users. Use OKRs for change you are pursuing and KPIs for the health you must maintain.

  9. North Star MetricThe single metric that best captures the value customers get, and that leads revenue.

    The north star is the single measure that best captures the value customers receive, such as weekly songs played per listener. It leads revenue and guides the team. Support it with input metrics the team can directly influence.

  10. Product Portfolio ManagementAllocating investment across multiple products or bets by stage, risk and return.

    Portfolio management allocates investment across products or bets by stage, risk and expected return. A healthy portfolio mixes proven products that fund growth with a few experiments that could become the next major line.

  11. Feature PrioritisationOrdering work by value, effort, risk and strategic fit with explicit criteria rather than opinion.

    Prioritisation orders work by value, effort, risk and strategic fit using explicit criteria. Write the criteria down so the ranking can be challenged on the inputs rather than on personal opinion.

  12. RICE FrameworkScore = (Reach x Impact x Confidence) / Effort, a simple way to compare initiatives.

    RICE scores an initiative as (Reach x Impact x Confidence) divided by Effort. Example: reaching 5,000 users with impact 2, confidence 80% and effort 2 person-months gives (5,000 x 2 x 0.8) / 2 = 4,000. Use the score to compare items, not to decide alone.

  13. MoSCoW FrameworkClassify requirements as Must, Should, Could and Won't have in this release to fix scope.

    MoSCoW sorts requirements into Must, Should, Could and Won't-have for this release. It fixes scope early. Teams often label too many items as Must, so ask what fails if the item is removed.

  14. Kano ModelSeparates basic expectations, performance features and delighters to see how features affect satisfaction.

    The Kano model separates basic expectations, performance features that scale satisfaction, and delighters that create surprise. Basic expectations must work or users leave. Delighters should be added after the basics are reliable.

  15. ICE ScoringImpact, Confidence and Ease scored 1-10 and multiplied or averaged for fast ranking.

    ICE scores Impact, Confidence and Ease from 1 to 10 and combines them. It is quick for early ideas. Its weakness is subjectivity, so calibrate scores by comparing two or three items the whole team already understands.

  16. Impact vs Effort MatrixA 2x2 that shows quick wins, big bets, fill-ins and time sinks.

    A two-by-two grid places items as quick wins (high impact, low effort), big bets, fill-ins and time sinks. It makes trade-offs visible in a single meeting. Plot items as a group discussion, not as one person's view.

  17. Opportunity ScoringRates how important a customer need is against how satisfied they are; high importance with low satisfaction signals opportunity.

    Opportunity scoring rates how important each customer need is and how satisfied customers are with current solutions. Needs that are important but poorly served point to opportunities. It works best with a survey of many customers.

  18. Stakeholder PrioritisationAligning priorities with executives, sales, support and engineering using shared criteria and transparent trade-offs.

    Stakeholders bring competing requests from sales, support and executives. Agree the criteria first, then score requests together. Show how each request ranks, so people see the trade-off rather than a decision made in private.

  19. Strategic Trade-OffsChoosing what not to do: speed vs quality, breadth vs depth, build now vs later, and documenting the reasoning.

    Strategy requires saying no. Trade-offs include speed against quality, breadth against depth and building now against later. Record the reasoning and the conditions under which the decision would change.

  20. Product Investment DecisionsDeciding where to put people and money using expected value, strategic fit, evidence and option value.

    Investment decisions weigh expected value, strategic fit, evidence and option value, which is the value of keeping choices open. Stage investment so a bet earns more funding only after it hits defined evidence milestones.

SUBJECT 04Product Design, UX and Prototyping17 topics

The design knowledge a PM needs to partner with designers, test ideas cheaply and shape usable, accessible products, including AI-first experiences.

  1. UX FundamentalsUser experience covers the whole interaction: usefulness, usability, findability, credibility and delight.

    UX covers the whole experience: usefulness, usability, findability, credibility and delight. A feature can be useful but hard to find, so the PM must consider each aspect. Start evaluation with whether the user can complete the core task.

  2. UI FundamentalsVisual interface design: layout, typography, colour, components and consistency.

    UI is the visual layer: layout, typography, colour, components and states such as hover and error. Consistent components reduce cognitive load. A design system keeps UI consistent across screens and teams.

  3. Human-Centred DesignDesigning with deep understanding of people and testing with them throughout.

    Human-centred design involves users throughout the process, from research to testing. It starts from user needs and checks solutions with real people. PMs contribute by bringing research findings into design discussions early.

  4. Design Thinking ProcessEmpathise, define, ideate, prototype, test, applied iteratively to product problems.

    The process of empathise, define, ideate, prototype and test is applied repeatedly to product problems. Each loop should narrow uncertainty. Stop when the riskiest assumptions are tested, not when the design looks finished.

  5. Information ArchitectureOrganising and labelling content and navigation so users can find things.

    Information architecture organises and labels content and navigation so users can find things. Good labels use the words users use, not internal jargon. Test labels with card sorting or tree tests before building menus.

  6. User FlowsDiagrams of the steps a user takes to complete a task, including decisions and error paths.

    A user flow diagrams the steps a user takes to complete a task, including decisions and error paths. Flows expose missing states, such as what happens when a payment fails. Review flows with engineers to catch dependencies early.

  7. WireframingLow-detail layout sketches that settle structure and content before visual design.

    Wireframes are low-detail layouts that settle structure and content before visual design. They keep discussion on function. Use boxes and labels, not colours and icons, so feedback focuses on the structure.

  8. Low-Fidelity and High-Fidelity PrototypesLow-fi (paper, boxes) tests ideas fast; high-fi looks and behaves like the real product for realistic testing.

    Low-fidelity prototypes (paper or simple boxes) test ideas quickly and cheaply. High-fidelity prototypes look and behave like the final product, and are better for testing realistic tasks. Match the fidelity to the question you need answered.

  9. Interactive PrototypingClickable prototypes that simulate flows so you can test with users before engineering builds.

    Interactive prototypes simulate flows so users can click through a task before engineering starts. Test the riskiest task first, for example a checkout step, and watch where users hesitate.

  10. Figma FundamentalsFrames, components, auto layout, variables and prototyping in the standard collaborative design tool.

    Figma is the common design tool. Learn frames, components, auto layout, variables and prototyping. Components keep designs consistent, and variables let you change colours or spacing in one place.

  11. Usability TestingWatching representative users attempt tasks, to find where they struggle, with five users per round revealing most issues.

    Usability testing watches representative users attempt tasks. Five users per round typically reveal most major usability issues. Record the task, the wrong turns and the words users use, then fix the most severe issues first.

  12. Design ValidationConfirming a design solves the problem with evidence from tests, metrics and feedback before scaling it.

    Design validation confirms that a design solves the problem using test results, metrics and feedback. Before scaling a design, define what success looks like, such as task completion above 80% without help.

  13. Accessibility and Inclusive DesignDesigning for people with different abilities and contexts, guided by WCAG: contrast, keyboard use, screen readers and clear language.

    Inclusive design serves people with different abilities and contexts. WCAG guidelines define contrast, keyboard access, screen reader support and clear language. Accessibility improves the product for everyone, not only for disabled users.

  14. Mobile-First Product DesignDesigning for small screens and constraints first, then extending to larger screens.

    Designing for the smallest screen first forces focus on essential content and actions. Then expand to larger screens. Many users in India access services mainly on low-end phones and slow networks, so design for those constraints first.

  15. Conversational UXDesigning dialogue: turn-taking, clarifying questions, tone, error recovery and graceful hand-off.

    Conversational design covers turn-taking, clarifying questions, tone, error recovery and hand-off to a human. A good assistant states its scope clearly and recovers gracefully when it misunderstands the user.

  16. AI-First User ExperienceExperiences built around AI capability from the start, with uncertainty, feedback and control designed in.

    AI-first design starts from what the AI does well and designs fallbacks for what it cannot do. It makes uncertainty, sources and user control visible so people can judge when to rely on the output.

  17. Human-AI Interaction DesignPatterns that set expectations, show confidence, let users correct the AI and keep them in control.

    These patterns set expectations, show confidence levels, let users correct the AI and keep them in control. Example: an AI draft is shown with editable fields and an undo option, not auto-sent.

SUBJECT 05Product Requirements and Documentation20 topics

Writing clear requirements and documents that engineers can build from and teams can trace, test and release.

  1. Product Requirement Document (PRD)The shared document describing the problem, users, goals, scope, requirements, metrics and launch plan.

    A PRD describes the problem, target users, goals, scope, requirements, success metrics and launch plan. It is a shared reference for teams, not a contract. Keep it short and link to research and designs rather than copying them in.

  2. Business Requirement Document (BRD)Business-level needs, objectives, scope and constraints, usually for stakeholders and sponsors.

    A BRD captures business needs, objectives, scope and constraints, usually for sponsors and finance. It answers why the business should invest. The PRD then explains what the product will do to deliver that business case.

  3. Functional RequirementsWhat the system must do: behaviours and features.

    Functional requirements describe what the system must do, such as "a user can reset a password by email". Write each one so it can be tested. Vague phrases such as "easy to use" are not functional requirements.

  4. Non-Functional RequirementsHow well it must do it: performance, security, reliability, scalability and compliance.

    These describe how well the system must perform: response time, availability, security, scalability and compliance. Quantify them, for example "99.9% uptime per month" or "search returns in under 300 ms for 95% of queries".

  5. Technical RequirementsConstraints and needs for architecture, integrations, data, platforms and infrastructure.

    Technical requirements cover architecture, integrations, data formats, platforms and infrastructure. Engineers should review them early, because they often change cost and timeline. Include constraints such as data residency rules.

  6. Product SpecificationsDetailed descriptions of behaviour, rules, data and interfaces sufficient for build and test.

    Specifications describe behaviour, rules, data and interfaces in enough detail to build and test the feature. Include edge cases, validation rules and examples. A good spec reduces questions during development.

  7. Epics, Features and User StoriesEpics are large goals, features are deliverable capabilities, stories are small user-centred slices a team can finish in a sprint.

    An epic is a large goal, a feature is a deliverable capability, and a user story is a small, user-centred slice that fits in one sprint. Example: "Faster payouts" as an epic, "Instant payout request" as a feature, and "As a seller, I can request a payout from my dashboard" as a story.

  8. User Story MappingArranging stories along the user journey to see the whole experience and slice releases.

    Story mapping arranges stories along the user journey, with the most important path at the top. Slice releases horizontally so each release delivers a usable end-to-end path rather than half of many features.

  9. Acceptance CriteriaSpecific, testable conditions that must be true for a story to be accepted, often in Given/When/Then form.

    Acceptance criteria are specific, testable conditions a story must meet. The Given/When/Then format is common: given a verified account, when the user requests a payout above the limit, then the system shows the limit message.

  10. Definition of ReadyThe checklist a story must meet before the team starts it, such as clear value, criteria and dependencies.

    The checklist a story must meet before the team starts work: clear value, acceptance criteria, dependencies identified and design available. It prevents half-understood work entering a sprint.

  11. Definition of DoneThe shared quality bar every item must meet to count as finished: tested, reviewed, documented, deployable.

    The shared quality bar every item must meet: tested, reviewed, documented, monitored and deployable. Without it, items are called done before they are safe to release.

  12. Use CasesStep-by-step descriptions of how an actor achieves a goal with the system, including alternatives.

    A use case describes how an actor achieves a goal with the system, including alternative paths and failures. Use cases are useful for complex workflows such as loan approval, where many roles and rules interact.

  13. Edge CasesRare or extreme inputs and states that can break flows, found by asking what happens when things are empty, huge, slow or invalid.

    Edge cases are rare or extreme inputs and states, such as an empty cart, a very large file or a timeout during payment. Ask what happens when inputs are empty, huge, slow or invalid. Many production bugs live in these cases.

  14. Error ScenariosHow the product behaves when things fail, with clear messages, recovery paths and logging.

    Error scenarios describe how the product behaves when something fails, including the message shown, the recovery path and what is logged. Good error handling tells the user what to do next.

  15. Product Backlog CreationA prioritised list of everything that might be built, each item with value and clear description.

    The backlog is a prioritised list of everything that might be built. Each item needs a clear description and an expected value. Keep the backlog honest by removing items that no longer matter.

  16. Backlog RefinementRegularly clarifying, splitting, estimating and ordering backlog items so the next sprints are ready.

    Refinement regularly clarifies, splits, estimates and orders backlog items so the next sprints are ready. Refine two to three sprints ahead. Items should be small enough to finish within one sprint.

  17. Release DocumentationRelease notes, change logs, runbooks and support guides that explain what changed and how to operate it.

    Release notes, change logs, runbooks and support guides explain what changed and how to operate it. Support teams need these before launch, not after the first customer complaint.

  18. Product Requirement TraceabilityLinking each requirement to its goal, design, tests and release to prove coverage and manage change.

    Traceability links each requirement to its goal, design, tests and release. It shows that every requirement is covered and helps assess the impact of a change. Use a simple table or tool, and keep it current.

  19. Technical DocumentationArchitecture notes, API docs and runbooks that let others build, integrate and maintain.

    Architecture notes, API references and runbooks let others build, integrate and support the product. Keep them next to the code so they are updated with it.

  20. Product Decision RecordsShort records of decisions, options considered, reasons and consequences, so context is not lost.

    A decision record notes the decision, the options considered, the reason and the expected consequences. Months later, people can see why a choice was made instead of re-arguing it.

SUBJECT 06Agile, Scrum and Product Delivery23 topics

How teams plan, build and ship iteratively, and the tools PMs use to coordinate delivery.

  1. Agile FundamentalsIterative delivery, frequent feedback and adapting to change over following a fixed plan.

    Agile delivers work in short iterations, gathers feedback quickly and adapts plans to what is learned. It replaces long fixed plans with frequent checkpoints. The PM's job is to keep the backlog ordered by outcome value.

  2. Agile ManifestoFour values and twelve principles favouring individuals, working software, collaboration and responding to change.

    The manifesto values individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration and responding to change. The twelve principles add practical guidance such as frequent delivery and sustainable pace. Use the values as a test for practices, not as a rulebook.

  3. Scrum FrameworkFixed-length sprints with defined roles, events and artefacts to deliver increments.

    Scrum organises work into fixed-length sprints, with defined roles, events and artefacts. Each sprint produces an increment of working product. Teams adopt Scrum to create a predictable rhythm of planning, review and improvement.

  4. Kanban MethodologyA continuous flow board that limits work in progress and optimises throughput.

    Kanban visualises work on a board, limits work in progress and measures flow. It suits support and operations work with unpredictable arrivals. Limits prevent teams from starting too much and finishing nothing.

  5. Scrum Roles and ResponsibilitiesProduct Owner, Scrum Master and Developers, each accountable for a distinct part of the work.

    The product owner orders the backlog and maximises value. The Scrum master removes impediments and coaches the process. Developers build the increment. Each role has a distinct accountability, and blurring them is a common failure.

  6. Product Owner ResponsibilitiesMaximising product value by managing and ordering the backlog and accepting work.

    The product owner manages the backlog, writes clear items, accepts work against criteria and represents customers to the team. A product owner who is absent from daily decisions cannot steer the team well.

  7. Sprint PlanningThe team selects backlog items and plans how to deliver them within the sprint goal.

    The team selects backlog items and agrees a sprint goal. Planning answers what can be delivered and how. A good sprint goal describes the outcome, so the team can adapt scope without losing purpose.

  8. Daily Stand-upsA 15-minute sync on progress toward the sprint goal and blockers.

    The daily stand-up is a 15-minute check on progress toward the sprint goal and on blockers. It is not a status report to the manager. Use it to remove blockers quickly, for example by escalating a waiting API decision the same day.

  9. Sprint ReviewsDemonstrating the increment to stakeholders and gathering feedback.

    The sprint review demonstrates the working increment to stakeholders and gathers feedback. It is the best place to show real software, not slides. Feedback here should change the backlog order.

  10. Sprint RetrospectivesThe team reflects on how it worked and agrees small improvements.

    The retrospective examines how the team worked and agrees small, specific improvements. Keep one or two actions per sprint and track whether they helped. Retrospectives fail when they produce long lists nobody follows.

  11. Backlog GroomingOlder term for refinement: keeping the backlog clear, sized and ordered.

    Backlog grooming is the older term for refinement: keeping items clear, sized and ordered. Groom continuously rather than in one large session, so the team is never starting from scratch.

  12. Story PointsA relative unit of effort and complexity for sizing stories, not hours.

    Story points measure relative size and complexity, not hours. A story of 5 points should feel about five times the effort of a 1-point story. Use points for forecasting, and do not compare teams by points.

  13. Estimation TechniquesPlanning poker, t-shirt sizing, affinity estimation and reference-class estimates to forecast work.

    Planning poker, t-shirt sizing and affinity estimation provide quick estimates. Reference-class estimates compare work to similar past items. Estimates are forecasts with error bars, so communicate ranges.

  14. Velocity and Burndown ChartsVelocity is points completed per sprint; burndown shows remaining work over time. Use for forecasting, not for judging people.

    Velocity is the points completed per sprint, and burndown shows remaining work over time. They forecast delivery. Using them to judge individuals damages trust and distorts estimates.

  15. Release PlanningSequencing sprints into releases with dates, scope, dependencies and risk buffers; staged rollout and rollback plans belong here.

    Release planning sequences sprints into releases with scope, dates, dependencies and risk buffers. Staged rollouts such as 5%, 25% and 100% of users, with rollback plans, belong here. A release is ready when its go/no-go criteria are met.

  16. Dependency ManagementIdentifying and tracking work that relies on other teams, systems or vendors, and resolving early.

    A dependency is work that relies on another team, system or vendor. List dependencies early, agree dates with those teams and track them in each sprint review. Hidden dependencies cause most missed release dates.

  17. Risk and Issue ManagementRisks are possible problems to mitigate; issues are problems that have occurred and need action.

    A risk is a possible problem you can prevent or reduce. An issue is a problem that has already happened and needs action. Keep a short risk register with owners, likelihood, impact and the mitigation date.

  18. Cross-Functional Team CollaborationProduct, design, engineering, data, QA and go-to-market working toward one goal with shared context.

    Product, design, engineering, data, QA and go-to-market work toward one goal with shared context. Shared goals and regular demos prevent the handoff delays of separate silos.

  19. Engineering CollaborationWorking with engineers on trade-offs, feasibility and scope, treating them as partners from discovery onward.

    Involve engineers from discovery onward. They know the cost of options and can suggest simpler solutions. Treat trade-offs on scope and quality as joint decisions.

  20. Product Delivery LifecycleIdea to discovery, design, build, test, release, measure and iterate.

    The lifecycle runs from idea to discovery, design, build, test, release, measurement and iteration. Measurement closes the loop: the results decide whether to invest more, change direction or stop.

  21. JiraIssue and sprint tracking tool for backlogs, boards, workflows and reports.

    Jira tracks issues, sprints, boards and workflows. Keep the workflow simple, with few statuses, and use a clear naming and labelling convention. Reports are useful only if the data is kept current.

  22. ConfluenceTeam wiki used for specs, decision records and knowledge sharing, integrated with Jira.

    Confluence is a team wiki for specifications, decision records and knowledge. Link pages to Jira issues so context stays connected to the work. Archive old pages so people can find current ones.

  23. Linear and Azure DevOpsLinear is a fast, opinionated issue tracker; Azure DevOps combines boards, repos and pipelines for engineering teams.

    Linear is a fast, opinionated issue tracker popular with product teams. Azure DevOps combines boards, repositories and pipelines for engineering organisations. Choose the tool that fits how your engineering team already works.

SUBJECT 07Product Analytics and Data-Driven Decision Making26 topics

The data skills a PM uses to answer questions, measure behaviour and run experiments that inform decisions.

  1. Data Fundamentals for Product ManagersData types, sources, quality, events vs tables, and the habit of asking what decision the data should inform.

    Data types include numbers, categories, timestamps and text. Events record what a user did, while tables store entities such as accounts. Before analysing anything, ask what decision the data should inform.

  2. Descriptive StatisticsMean, median, mode, spread and percentiles to summarise data; prefer medians and percentiles for skewed metrics.

    Mean, median, mode, spread and percentiles summarise data. Use medians and percentiles for skewed measures such as session length, because a few very long sessions distort the mean.

  3. Probability FundamentalsLikelihood, conditional probability and distributions, the basis for reasoning under uncertainty.

    Probability measures how likely an outcome is. Conditional probability asks how likely an outcome is given another event, such as churn given a failed payment. Distributions describe the range of possible values for a metric.

  4. Hypothesis TestingTesting whether an observed difference is likely due to chance, using null hypothesis, p-values and significance level.

    Hypothesis testing asks whether an observed difference is likely due to chance. The null hypothesis states there is no difference. A p-value below a pre-set threshold, often 0.05, suggests the difference is unlikely to be chance, but it does not measure business importance.

  5. SQL FundamentalsSELECT, WHERE, GROUP BY, ORDER BY and LIMIT to query tables.

    SELECT, WHERE, GROUP BY, ORDER BY and LIMIT let you query tables. Example: SELECT city, COUNT(*) FROM orders WHERE status = 'paid' GROUP BY city returns paid orders per city.

  6. SQL Joins and AggregationsCombining tables (inner, left) and summarising with COUNT, SUM, AVG across groups.

    Joins combine tables on keys. Inner joins keep matches only; left joins keep all rows from the left table. Aggregations such as COUNT, SUM and AVG summarise groups. Check row counts after each join to avoid double counting.

  7. Advanced SQL for Product AnalyticsWindow functions, CTEs, date handling and cohort queries for retention and funnel analysis.

    Window functions, common table expressions and date functions answer product questions such as "what share of users returned on day 7?" Build cohort tables step by step and check each intermediate result.

  8. Excel and Google SheetsPivot tables, lookups, formulas and charts for fast analysis and sharing.

    Pivot tables, lookups, formulas and charts are fast tools for analysis and sharing. Use them for quick exploration and small datasets. Move to SQL and a BI tool when data grows or needs refreshing.

  9. Data VisualisationChoosing charts that make comparisons obvious, with clear labels and no distortion.

    Choose charts that make the comparison obvious: bars for categories, lines for trends. Label axes clearly, start bars at zero and avoid decorative effects that distort the message.

  10. Tableau and Power BIBI tools for interactive dashboards and self-serve reporting on warehouse data.

    These BI tools build interactive dashboards and self-serve reports on warehouse data. Good dashboards show a small set of decisions and the metric definitions beside them, so people interpret the numbers the same way.

  11. Product Analytics FundamentalsTracking user behaviour as events with properties, then analysing funnels, cohorts and paths.

    Product analytics records user behaviour as events with properties, then analyses funnels, cohorts and paths. Clean event names and a tracking plan prevent confusing results later.

  12. Funnel AnalysisMeasuring conversion between steps to find where users drop off.

    A funnel measures conversion between steps, such as visit, signup, activation and purchase. The biggest drop is a starting point for investigation, not an answer. Segment the funnel by source and device to see who drops off.

  13. Cohort AnalysisGrouping users by a shared start (such as signup week) and following them over time.

    A cohort groups users by a shared start, such as signup week. Following each cohort shows whether newer users behave better or worse than older ones over time.

  14. Retention AnalysisHow many users return over days or weeks; the retention curve flattening signals product-market fit.

    Retention shows how many users return over days or weeks. A retention curve that flattens at a healthy level is a common sign of product-market fit, while a curve that keeps falling signals a problem.

  15. Churn AnalysisWhy and when customers leave, using exit surveys, usage patterns and segment comparison.

    Churn analysis asks why and when customers leave, using exit surveys, usage before cancellation and segment comparisons. Churn usually follows a clear drop in usage, so track engagement as an early warning.

  16. Conversion Rate OptimisationImproving the share of visitors who complete a goal through research, tests and friction removal.

    CRO improves the share of visitors who complete a goal through research, testing and friction removal. Start with the steps where users stall, then test changes against a clear hypothesis.

  17. Customer SegmentationGrouping users by behaviour, value or needs to tailor products and messages.

    Segments group users by behaviour, value or need. A segment is useful when it suggests a different product choice or message. Segments based only on demographics often fail to change any decision.

  18. A/B TestingRandomly split users between versions and compare a pre-chosen metric to measure causal impact.

    An A/B test randomly splits users between two versions and compares a pre-chosen metric. Randomisation makes the difference causal. Decide the sample size and duration before starting, and do not stop early because one result looks good.

  19. Multivariate TestingTesting combinations of multiple elements at once; needs more traffic than A/B.

    Multivariate tests vary several elements at once to find the best combination. They need far more traffic than A/B tests. Use them only when you have enough users and a clear reason to test interactions.

  20. Experimentation FrameworksA repeatable process: hypothesis, metric, sample size, guardrails, run length, analysis and decision.

    A framework sets a repeatable process: hypothesis, primary metric, guardrails, sample size, run length, analysis and decision rule. Teams that follow one framework make fewer false conclusions.

  21. Event TrackingDefining and logging user actions with a consistent naming plan so analysis is trustworthy.

    Event tracking defines and logs user actions with a consistent naming plan, for example "checkout_started" with properties. Review the plan when features change so historical data remains comparable.

  22. Product Analytics DashboardsLive views of key metrics for the team, with a clear owner, definitions and thresholds for action.

    A dashboard shows key metrics for the team, with a clear owner, definitions and thresholds that trigger action. A dashboard without an owner tends to be ignored within months.

  23. MixpanelEvent-based product analytics for funnels, retention and cohorts.

    Mixpanel is event-based analytics for funnels, retention and cohorts. It suits teams that want quick self-serve analysis of user behaviour in web and mobile products.

  24. AmplitudeProduct analytics platform with behavioural cohorts, journeys and experimentation.

    Amplitude offers behavioural cohorts, user journeys and experimentation tools. Like Mixpanel, it works from events, so the quality of the tracking plan determines the quality of the analysis.

  25. Google AnalyticsWeb and app analytics for traffic, acquisition and conversion.

    Google Analytics measures web and app traffic, acquisition channels and conversions. It is a common starting point for marketing data. Its event model and reports differ from product analytics tools, so check definitions before comparing numbers.

  26. Data-Driven Product DecisionsCombining quantitative data with qualitative insight, stating the decision rule in advance.

    Combine quantitative data with qualitative insight. Write the decision rule before looking at results, for example "we will launch if activation rises by at least 3 points without lowering revenue per user". This prevents rationalising the result afterward.

SUBJECT 08Product Metrics and Business Analytics20 topics

The metrics that describe product health and business performance, and how to read and review them.

  1. Product KPIsA small set of measures tied to goals that show whether the product is succeeding.

    Product KPIs are a small set of measures tied to goals that show whether the product is succeeding. Pick five or fewer, and define each one precisely, including the numerator, denominator, time window and filters.

  2. North Star MetricsThe one measure of delivered customer value that guides the whole team, supported by input metrics.

    The north star is the one measure of the value customers receive, such as completed bookings per week for a travel app. It should lead revenue and be supported by input metrics the teams can influence.

  3. Acquisition MetricsHow users arrive: visitors, signups, cost per acquisition and channel conversion.

    Acquisition metrics show how users arrive: visitors, signups, cost per acquisition and conversion by channel. Compare channels on the quality of the users they bring, not only the volume.

  4. Activation MetricsWhether new users reach the first moment of value, such as completing a key action.

    Activation measures whether new users reach their first moment of value, such as sending a first invoice. Define the activation event from data showing which early actions predict long-term retention.

  5. Engagement MetricsHow deeply and often users use the product: sessions, actions, time in product, stickiness.

    Engagement measures how deeply and often users use the product: sessions, key actions, time in product and stickiness. Engagement should be tied to value, because time spent alone can reflect confusion.

  6. Retention MetricsThe share of users or revenue that remains over time.

    Retention is the share of users or revenue that remains over time. Report it by cohort and over several time periods, so changes in product quality are visible.

  7. Revenue MetricsRevenue, ARPU, expansion and contraction, margin and bookings.

    Revenue metrics include total revenue, average revenue per user, expansion and contraction, margin and bookings. Separate recurring and one-time revenue to see the health of the subscription base.

  8. Customer Satisfaction MetricsDirect measures of how customers feel about the product or interaction.

    These capture how customers feel about the product or an interaction. Satisfaction is a lagging signal, so pair it with behavioural metrics to see whether opinions match actions.

  9. NPS, CSAT and CESNPS asks likelihood to recommend; CSAT rates satisfaction with an interaction; CES measures how easy a task was.

    NPS asks how likely people are to recommend the product, CSAT rates satisfaction with an interaction, and CES measures how much effort a task took. Use CES after support interactions and NPS for relationship health.

  10. DAU, WAU and MAUDaily, weekly and monthly active users; DAU/MAU indicates stickiness.

    Daily, weekly and monthly active users count distinct users. The DAU/MAU ratio indicates stickiness. A product used once a month needs different measures than one used every day.

  11. ARPU and ARPPUAverage revenue per user and per paying user.

    ARPU is average revenue per user across all users, and ARPPU is average revenue per paying user. Comparing them shows how much of the change comes from conversion to paid versus price or upsell.

  12. Customer Lifetime Value (LTV)Expected gross profit from a customer over their lifetime, from average revenue, margin and lifespan.

    LTV is the expected gross profit from a customer over their relationship with the product. A simple version multiplies average monthly gross profit by average lifespan in months. Use it with CAC to decide how much acquisition spending is sustainable.

  13. Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)Total sales and marketing spend divided by customers acquired in the period.

    CAC is total sales and marketing spend divided by the number of new customers acquired in the same period. Track it by channel, and be clear about whether it includes sales salaries.

  14. LTV/CAC RatioReturn on acquisition spend; around 3 or higher is often a healthy target for subscription businesses.

    LTV divided by CAC shows the return on acquisition spending. A ratio around 3 is often cited as healthy for subscription businesses, but the benchmark depends on payback period and cash position.

  15. Monthly Recurring Revenue (MRR)Predictable subscription revenue normalised to a month.

    MRR is predictable subscription revenue normalised to a month. Track its components: new, expansion, contraction and churned MRR. The movement between them explains growth or decline.

  16. Annual Recurring Revenue (ARR)Predictable subscription revenue annualised, typically MRR x 12.

    ARR annualises recurring revenue, typically MRR multiplied by 12. It is useful for comparing subscription businesses, but it must exclude one-time fees and be defined consistently.

  17. Churn and Retention RateChurn is the share lost in a period; retention is the share kept, and the two sum to one.

    Churn is the share of customers or revenue lost in a period, and retention is the share kept. They sum to 100% over the same base. Report logo churn and revenue churn separately, since large customers can distort revenue figures.

  18. Feature AdoptionThe share of eligible users who try and keep using a feature.

    Feature adoption is the share of eligible users who try a feature and keep using it. A launched feature used by 2% of users after three months needs investigation into discovery, value or fit.

  19. Product Health MetricsA balanced set: reliability, performance, quality, support load, satisfaction and usage.

    A balanced set includes reliability, performance, quality, support load, satisfaction and usage. Health metrics warn of problems before growth metrics decline.

  20. Product Performance ReviewsA regular forum to read metrics, explain changes, agree actions and adjust the roadmap.

    A regular review reads metrics, explains changes, agrees actions and adjusts the roadmap. Each review should end with named owners and dates for the actions it creates.

SUBJECT 09Business, Finance and Product Commercialisation24 topics

How products make money and how a PM builds a financial case, sets price and takes the product to market.

  1. Business FundamentalsRevenue, cost, profit, cash flow, margins and how value flows through a company.

    A business earns revenue and pays costs to make profit. Cash flow timing matters as much as profit, because a growing business can still run out of cash. Product decisions change revenue, cost and cash, so PMs need this vocabulary.

  2. Business Model CanvasNine blocks describing customers, value, channels, revenue, resources, activities, partners and costs.

    The canvas has nine blocks: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners and cost structure. It helps check that the parts of a business fit together.

  3. Revenue ModelsSubscription, transaction fee, advertising, licensing, usage-based, marketplace commission and services.

    Common models include subscription, transaction fee, advertising, licensing, usage-based pricing, marketplace commission and services. Each model shapes what the product should optimise for.

  4. Subscription Business ModelsRecurring fees for continued access, optimising retention, expansion and predictable revenue.

    Customers pay a recurring fee for continued access. Revenue is predictable, but retention becomes the main lever. Improving onboarding and value delivery matters as much as acquisition.

  5. Freemium ModelsA free tier drives adoption and conversion to paid; works when marginal cost is low and upgrade triggers are clear.

    A free tier drives adoption and conversion to paid plans. It works when the cost of serving free users is low and the upgrade trigger is clear, such as a limit that users hit when they are already getting value.

  6. Usage-Based PricingCustomers pay according to consumption, aligning price with value but making revenue less predictable.

    Customers pay according to consumption, such as API calls or messages sent. The price matches value, but revenue is less predictable, so forecasting and customer budgets need extra attention.

  7. Product Pricing StrategySetting price using cost, competitors and value to the customer, with packaging and tiers that fit segments.

    Pricing uses cost, competitor prices and the value to the customer. Packaging into tiers fits different segments. Test price changes on new customers first, because changing prices for existing customers creates churn risk.

  8. Monetisation StrategiesThe mechanisms by which a product earns revenue and when in the user journey they appear.

    These are the mechanisms by which a product earns money and when in the journey they appear. A paywall shown before any value is delivered often reduces activation.

  9. Financial ModellingA spreadsheet model of revenue, costs and cash under stated assumptions, with scenarios.

    A financial model is a spreadsheet of revenue, costs and cash under stated assumptions. Build best, base and worst scenarios and state which assumptions matter most, so the model supports decisions rather than hiding risk.

  10. Unit EconomicsProfit or loss per unit (customer or transaction) after variable costs, such as CAC, LTV and payback period.

    Unit economics measures profit or loss per customer or transaction after variable costs. Key measures are CAC, LTV and payback period. Healthy unit economics mean each customer pays back acquisition cost within an acceptable time.

  11. ROI and ROEReturn on investment is gain relative to cost; return on equity is net income relative to shareholder equity.

    ROI is the gain from an investment divided by its cost. ROE is net income divided by shareholder equity. For product work, ROI is the more common tool to compare investment options.

  12. Cost-Benefit AnalysisComparing total expected costs and benefits, in money terms, to decide.

    This compares total expected costs and benefits in money terms, including time saved and risk reduced. Quantify benefits conservatively and show the assumptions, so the comparison is credible.

  13. Break-Even AnalysisThe volume at which revenue equals total cost; fixed cost divided by contribution margin per unit.

    Break-even is the volume at which revenue equals total cost. It equals fixed costs divided by contribution margin per unit. It shows how many customers a new product must win to stop losing money.

  14. Profitability AnalysisAssessing margins by product, segment or channel to see where money is made.

    Profitability analysis looks at margins by product, segment or channel to see where money is made or lost. A profitable product line can hide an unprofitable segment that drives support costs.

  15. Business Case DevelopmentA structured argument for an investment: problem, options, costs, benefits, risks and recommendation.

    A business case argues for an investment: problem, options, costs, benefits, risks and a recommendation. Lead with the decision requested. Include the cost of doing nothing.

  16. Budget PlanningAllocating funds to teams and initiatives, tracking against plan and forecasting.

    Budget planning allocates funds to teams and initiatives, tracks spending against plan and forecasts the rest of the year. Keep a reserve for unexpected work, such as an urgent compliance change.

  17. Go-to-Market StrategyWho you sell to, with what message and price, through which channels, and how you measure launch.

    The GTM strategy defines who the product is for, the message, the price, the channels and how success will be measured after launch. It connects product decisions with sales and marketing.

  18. Product Launch StrategyA staged plan for readiness, beta, announcement, enablement and post-launch monitoring.

    A launch plan covers readiness checks, a beta with selected users, announcement, sales enablement, support preparation and post-launch monitoring. The launch date should follow the readiness checklist, not the other way round.

  19. Product AdoptionMoving customers from awareness to trial to regular use through onboarding and value moments.

    Adoption moves customers from awareness to trial to regular use. Onboarding, first success and reminders at the right moments drive adoption more than announcements.

  20. Customer AcquisitionChannels and tactics to attract customers efficiently: content, paid, partners, sales and referrals.

    Acquisition channels include content, paid media, partners, sales and referrals. Compare channels by cost, quality of customers and payback. A channel that brings many customers who never activate is expensive.

  21. Customer Retention StrategyKeeping customers through onboarding, ongoing value, support, community and proactive outreach.

    Retention comes from onboarding, ongoing value, support, community and proactive outreach to users who show declining engagement. Retention improvements often pay back faster than new acquisition.

  22. Product Growth StrategyLoops and levers across acquisition, activation, retention, revenue and referral.

    Growth strategy identifies loops and levers across acquisition, activation, retention, revenue and referral. Choose the lever with the largest gap in the funnel and test changes against it.

  23. Product-Led SalesUsing product usage signals to identify and convert qualified accounts, blending self-serve with sales.

    Product usage signals identify accounts ready to buy. Sales then engages those accounts with context from the product. It blends self-serve adoption with human sales for larger deals.

  24. Enterprise Product CommercialisationSelling to large organisations: security reviews, procurement, pilots, integration and long sales cycles.

    Selling to large organisations involves security reviews, procurement, pilots, integration and long sales cycles. Plan these steps into the product roadmap, such as single sign-on and audit logs, because they often decide the deal.

SUBJECT 10Technical Foundations for Product Managers28 topics

Enough software and systems knowledge to discuss trade-offs credibly with engineers and make sound decisions.

  1. How Software Applications WorkUsers interact with a front end that calls back-end services, which read and write data and return results.

    A user interacts with a front end, which sends requests to back-end services. Those services apply business rules, read and write data and return results. Knowing this flow helps a PM describe where a bug or delay happens.

  2. Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC)Plan, design, build, test, deploy and maintain, in sequential or iterative form.

    The SDLC covers planning, design, build, test, deploy and maintain. It can run in sequence or iteratively. Each stage has outputs such as requirements, designs, code and release notes that the PM reviews.

  3. Frontend FundamentalsHTML, CSS and JavaScript in the browser or native code on mobile, handling what users see and touch.

    Frontend code (HTML, CSS and JavaScript on the web, or native code on mobile) controls what users see and touch. Performance on frontend depends on page weight, rendering and network requests.

  4. Backend FundamentalsServer logic, business rules, databases and integrations that power the front end.

    The back end runs server logic, business rules, databases and integrations. Latency and reliability problems often start here, so PMs should understand what runs on the back end and what each request costs.

  5. Client-Server ArchitectureClients send requests; servers process and respond, over a network.

    Clients send requests and servers process them and send responses over a network. Most apps follow this pattern. Consider where data is processed when designing offline or low-bandwidth features.

  6. Databases and Data StoragePersistent stores for application data, chosen for structure, scale and query needs.

    Databases persist application data. The choice depends on structure, scale, consistency and query needs. Changing a database later is expensive, so involve engineers early in data decisions.

  7. SQL and NoSQL DatabasesSQL stores structured tables with strong consistency; NoSQL covers document, key-value, column and graph stores built for flexibility and scale.

    SQL databases store structured tables with strong consistency and joins. NoSQL covers document, key-value, column and graph stores that trade some consistency or queryability for flexibility and scale.

  8. Relational Database ConceptsTables, keys, relationships, normalisation and transactions (ACID).

    Relational databases use tables, keys, relationships and normalisation. Transactions follow ACID properties (atomicity, consistency, isolation, durability), which keep money movements correct.

  9. APIs and API FundamentalsContracts that let software components talk, with defined requests, responses and rules.

    An API is a contract that lets software components communicate. It defines requests, responses, authentication and error behaviour. Good APIs are stable and documented, because other teams depend on them.

  10. REST APIsResource-based HTTP APIs using verbs like GET, POST, PUT and DELETE and status codes.

    REST exposes resources through HTTP verbs such as GET, POST, PUT and DELETE, and uses status codes to report results. Example: GET /orders/123 returns one order.

  11. GraphQLA query language where clients request exactly the fields they need from one endpoint.

    GraphQL lets clients request exactly the fields they need from a single endpoint. It reduces over-fetching for complex screens but needs care with query cost and caching.

  12. WebhooksEvent-driven callbacks where one system sends an HTTP request to another when something happens.

    A webhook sends an HTTP request from one system to another when an event occurs, such as payment succeeded. It avoids constant polling, but the receiver must handle retries and duplicate events.

  16. PostmanA tool to explore, test and document APIs by sending requests and viewing responses.

    Postman lets you send requests, inspect responses and save collections of API calls. PMs use it to understand how an API behaves before writing requirements.

  17. Git and GitHub FundamentalsGit tracks changes; GitHub hosts repositories and supports pull requests and reviews.

    Git tracks changes to code. GitHub hosts repositories and supports pull requests and code review. Knowing the basic workflow lets a PM follow release discussions and documentation changes.

  18. Version ControlRecording history of changes so teams can collaborate, branch, review and roll back.

    Version control records history so teams can collaborate, branch, review and roll back. Rollbacks depend on it, and it is the audit trail for what changed in each release.

  19. Microservices ArchitectureMany small, independently deployable services that communicate over APIs.

    Microservices split an application into small, independently deployable services that talk over APIs. They help large teams work in parallel, but add operational complexity and network failure modes.

  20. Monolithic ArchitectureOne deployable unit containing all functionality: simple to start, harder to scale teams.

    A monolith is one deployable unit containing all functionality. It is simpler to start and debug, but large teams can slow each other down. Many successful products begin as monoliths.

  21. System Design FundamentalsChoosing components, data flow and trade-offs to meet requirements for scale, cost and reliability.

    System design chooses components, data flows and trade-offs to meet requirements for scale, cost and reliability. PMs do not design systems alone, but they should know which trade-offs their requirements create.

  22. Scalability and ReliabilityHandling growth in load and staying available despite failures, using redundancy, caching and graceful degradation.

SUBJECT 11Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning Foundations32 topics

How AI and machine learning work, the main problem types and how models are trained and judged.

  1. Introduction to Artificial IntelligenceSystems that perform tasks associated with human intelligence, such as perception, language and decision making.

    AI refers to systems that perform tasks associated with human intelligence, such as perceiving images, understanding language and making decisions. Most AI in products today is machine learning, which learns patterns from data.

  2. History and Evolution of AIRules and symbolic AI, expert systems, statistical ML, deep learning from 2012, transformers from 2017, and generative AI.

    The field moved from rule-based and symbolic systems to expert systems, then statistical machine learning, deep learning from around 2012, transformer models from 2017 and today's generative models. Each shift changed what products could do.

  3. Traditional AI vs Generative AITraditional models classify or predict from data; generative models create new text, images, audio or code.

    Traditional models classify or predict from data, such as flagging fraud. Generative models create new text, images, audio or code. Traditional AI outputs a label or number; generative AI outputs content that needs review.

  4. Narrow AI vs General AINarrow AI does specific tasks; general AI would match human breadth, and does not yet exist.

    Narrow AI performs specific tasks well, such as translation or recommendation. General AI would match human breadth across tasks. Only narrow AI exists today, so products should be designed around specific tasks.

  5. AI Applications Across IndustriesFraud detection, recommendations, diagnostics, document processing, support automation, forecasting and more.

    Examples include fraud detection in banking, recommendations in e-commerce, diagnostics in healthcare, document processing in insurance, support automation and demand forecasting in retail.

  6. Machine Learning FundamentalsAlgorithms that learn patterns from data to predict or decide without explicit rules.

    Machine learning algorithms learn patterns from data to predict or decide without explicit rules for every case. Quality depends mostly on data quality and on defining the target correctly.

  7. Supervised LearningLearning from labelled examples to predict labels for new data.

    Supervised learning trains on labelled examples to predict labels for new data. Example: historical loan applications labelled repaid or defaulted, used to predict risk for a new applicant.

  8. Unsupervised LearningFinding structure in unlabelled data, such as clusters or anomalies.

    Unsupervised learning finds structure in unlabelled data, such as clusters of similar customers or unusual transactions. Results need interpretation, because no correct answer is given.

  9. Semi-Supervised LearningUsing a small labelled set plus a large unlabelled set.

    It uses a small labelled dataset together with a large unlabelled one. This helps when labelling is expensive, such as medical images labelled only by specialists.

  10. Reinforcement LearningAn agent learns by trial and error to maximise cumulative reward.

    An agent learns by trial and error to maximise cumulative reward. It suits sequential decisions such as pricing or robot control, but needs a safe simulation or careful rollout.

  11. ClassificationPredicting a category, such as spam or not spam.

    Classification predicts a category, such as spam or not spam, or churn or stay. Its output is usually a probability, which the product converts into a decision using a threshold.

  12. RegressionPredicting a continuous number, such as price or demand.

    Regression predicts a continuous number, such as delivery time or demand next week. Errors are measured as average distance from the true value.

  13. ClusteringGrouping similar items without labels, used for segmentation.

    Clustering groups similar items without labels. It is used for customer segmentation and for finding natural groups in support tickets. Clusters must be named and checked by people.

  14. Recommendation SystemsSuggesting items using collaborative filtering, content features or hybrid models.

    Recommenders suggest items using collaborative filtering (what similar users chose), content features, or hybrid models. They need careful evaluation for diversity and for avoiding narrow feedback loops.

  15. Predictive AnalyticsUsing historical data to forecast outcomes such as churn or demand.

    Predictive analytics uses historical data to forecast outcomes such as churn, demand or default. A forecast is only useful if someone acts on it, so define the intervention before building the model.

  16. Feature EngineeringCreating informative inputs from raw data to help models learn.

    Feature engineering creates informative inputs from raw data, such as days since last purchase. Good features often matter more than a more complex algorithm.

  17. Training, Validation and TestingTrain to fit, validate to tune, and test once on unseen data for an honest estimate.

    The model is trained on one set, tuned on a validation set and evaluated once on an unseen test set. Keeping the test set untouched gives an honest estimate of performance.

  18. Model Training and InferenceTraining learns parameters from data; inference uses the trained model to make predictions.

    Training learns model parameters from data. Inference uses the trained model to make predictions on new inputs. Inference cost and latency matter in production even when training is done once.

  19. Overfitting and UnderfittingOverfitting memorises training noise and fails on new data; underfitting is too simple to capture the pattern.

    Overfitting memorises training noise and performs poorly on new data. Underfitting is too simple to capture the pattern. Compare training and validation performance to detect either.

  20. Bias-Variance Trade-OffSimple models have high bias; complex models have high variance; aim for the balance with best generalisation.

    Simple models have high bias and miss patterns. Complex models have high variance and react to noise. The aim is the level of complexity that generalises best to new data.

  21. Model Performance MetricsAccuracy, precision, recall, F1, AUC for classification; MAE and RMSE for regression, picked to match business cost of errors.

    Classification uses accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and AUC. Regression uses mean absolute error and root mean squared error. Pick the metric that matches the business cost of each kind of error.

  22. Precision, Recall and F1 ScorePrecision is correctness of positive predictions; recall is coverage of actual positives; F1 balances both.

    Precision is the share of positive predictions that are correct. Recall is the share of actual positives found. F1 combines both. In fraud detection, recall often matters more; in automatic account blocking, precision matters more.

  23. Confusion MatrixA table of true/false positives and negatives that shows the types of errors.

    A confusion matrix tabulates true positives, false positives, true negatives and false negatives. It shows what kinds of mistakes a model makes, which a single accuracy number hides.

  24. ROC-AUCArea under the curve of true-positive vs false-positive rate across thresholds; 0.5 is chance, 1.0 is perfect.

    The ROC curve plots true-positive rate against false-positive rate across thresholds. AUC is the area under it: 0.5 means chance and 1.0 means perfect ranking.

  25. Deep Learning FundamentalsMulti-layer neural networks that learn features automatically from large data.

    Deep learning uses multi-layer neural networks that learn features directly from large data, such as images or text. It needs more data and compute than classical methods.

  26. Neural NetworksLayers of connected units with weights, trained by backpropagation and gradient descent.

    Neural networks connect layers of units with weights. Training adjusts the weights with backpropagation and gradient descent to reduce error.

  27. CNN, RNN and LSTMCNNs process images, RNNs process sequences, LSTMs handle longer dependencies in sequences.

    CNNs process images using local patterns. RNNs process sequences step by step. LSTMs handle longer dependencies in sequences. Transformers have largely replaced RNNs for language tasks.

  28. TransformersAn architecture based on attention that processes sequences in parallel and underlies modern language models.

    Transformers process sequences in parallel using attention. They underpin modern language and multimodal models, and they scale well with data and compute.

  29. Attention MechanismsA way for a model to weigh which parts of the input matter for each output.

    Attention lets the model weigh which parts of the input matter for each output. It is the reason models can relate a word to a distant word in the same document.

  30. Natural Language ProcessingTechniques for understanding and generating human language, from tokenisation to LLMs.

    NLP covers understanding and generating human language, from tokenisation and classification to question answering. Large language models now handle most NLP tasks in products.

  31. Computer Vision FundamentalsTechniques for classifying, detecting and segmenting content in images and video.

    Computer vision classifies, detects and segments content in images and video. Applications include document scanning, quality inspection and product tagging.

  32. AI Model LifecycleProblem framing, data, training, evaluation, deployment, monitoring and retraining.

    The lifecycle covers problem framing, data collection, training, evaluation, deployment, monitoring and retraining. The PM owns framing, the success definition and the monitoring plan.

SUBJECT 12Generative AI and Large Language Models30 topics

What generative models are, how LLMs work, and the practical limits that shape product decisions.

  1. Introduction to Generative AIModels that generate new content from learned patterns, across text, image, audio, video and code.

    Generative AI models create new content such as text, images, audio, video and code, based on patterns learned from data. Its outputs are probabilistic, so the same prompt can give different answers.

  2. Generative AI ArchitectureThe stack from foundation model to prompts, retrieval, tools, guardrails and application layer.

    A typical stack includes the foundation model, prompts, retrieval of company data, tools the model can call, guardrails and the application layer that the user sees. Each layer can fail, so each needs monitoring.

  3. Foundation ModelsLarge models pre-trained on broad data and adapted to many tasks.

    Foundation models are large models pre-trained on broad data and adapted to many tasks. Products build on them through prompting, retrieval and fine-tuning rather than training from scratch.

  4. Large Language Models (LLMs)Transformer models trained to predict the next token, which gives them broad language ability.

    LLMs are transformer models trained to predict the next token in text. That simple objective, at scale and with alignment training, gives broad language ability, including summarising and coding.

  5. GPT ArchitectureA decoder-only transformer trained to predict the next token, scaled up and then aligned with instructions and feedback.

    GPT models are decoder-only transformers trained to predict the next token, then aligned with instructions and human feedback. The name refers to a family of OpenAI models; the architecture idea is shared widely.

  6. Claude Model EcosystemAnthropic's model family across capability and cost tiers, with long context, tool use and strong coding and writing; check current model names in its documentation.

    Anthropic offers a family of Claude models across capability and cost tiers, with long context windows and strong performance on writing and coding. Model names and prices change, so check the current documentation before choosing.

  7. Gemini Model EcosystemGoogle's multimodal model family for text, image, audio, video and code, offered through the Gemini API and Vertex AI.

    Google's Gemini models handle text, images, audio, video and code, and are available through the Gemini API and Vertex AI. Their multimodal inputs suit document and video workflows.

  8. Open-Source LLMsModels with downloadable weights, such as Llama, Mistral and Qwen, that you can host and tune, with licence terms to check.

    Models with downloadable weights, such as Llama, Mistral and Qwen, can be hosted and tuned by the company. Check the licence, because some restrict commercial use or large-scale deployment.

  9. Transformer ArchitectureStacked attention and feed-forward layers with embeddings and positional information.

    A transformer stacks attention and feed-forward layers, with embeddings and positional information. It processes all tokens in parallel, which makes training at scale practical.

  10. Tokens and TokenisationText is split into sub-word tokens; cost, limits and speed are measured in tokens.

    Text is split into sub-word tokens. Cost, context limits and speed are measured in tokens, so a 1,000-word document is roughly 1,300 to 1,500 tokens in English, and more in some other languages.

  11. Context WindowsThe maximum tokens a model can consider at once, covering prompt and response.

    The context window is the maximum number of tokens the model can consider at once, covering the prompt and the response. Long documents may need retrieval rather than being pasted in full.

  12. EmbeddingsNumeric vectors that capture meaning so similar items sit close together.

    Embeddings are numeric vectors that capture meaning, so similar texts sit close together. They power semantic search, recommendations and clustering.

  13. Pre-TrainingInitial large-scale training on broad data to learn language and world knowledge.

    Pre-training trains the model on very large general data to learn language and world knowledge. It is expensive and done by model providers, not by most product teams.

  14. Instruction TuningTraining on instruction-response pairs so the model follows directions.

    Instruction tuning trains the model on instruction and response pairs so it follows directions. It turns a text predictor into an assistant.

  15. Fine-TuningFurther training on task or domain data to change behaviour, style or accuracy.

    Fine-tuning trains a model further on task or domain data to change behaviour, style or accuracy on a narrow task. It is often unnecessary when good prompting and retrieval work.

  16. RLHFReinforcement learning from human feedback: human preferences train a reward signal to align outputs.

    Reinforcement learning from human feedback trains a reward model on human preferences and optimises the model against it. It makes outputs more helpful and safer, but it can also make them overly cautious.

  17. Reasoning ModelsModels that spend extra compute thinking through steps before answering, improving hard tasks at higher cost and latency.

    Reasoning models spend extra computation working through steps before answering. They improve on hard maths, planning and coding, but cost more and respond more slowly.

  18. Multimodal AIModels that handle several input or output types, such as text, images and audio.

    Multimodal models accept or produce several types of data, such as text, images and audio. A product can let users photograph a receipt and ask a question about it.

  19. Text GenerationProducing written content, summaries, answers, code and structured data.

    Text generation produces summaries, drafts, answers, code and structured data. Quality depends on the prompt, the context and the evaluation applied to the output.

  20. Image GenerationCreating images from prompts using diffusion or similar models.

    Image models create pictures from prompts, often using diffusion methods. Commercial use depends on the provider's licence and on the rights of training data, so check terms.

  21. Audio GenerationGenerating speech, music or sound effects.

    Audio models generate speech, music and sound effects. Voice cloning raises consent and misuse risks that the product must control.

  22. Video GenerationCreating video clips from text or images; quality, length and cost are still constraints.

    Video models create clips from text or images. Quality, length and cost remain constraints, so products usually limit duration and review outputs before publishing.

  23. Speech-to-TextTranscribing audio into text; accuracy depends on accents, noise and vocabulary.

    Speech recognition converts audio to text. Accuracy depends on accents, background noise and domain vocabulary, so test with real recordings from your users.

  24. Text-to-SpeechConverting text into natural-sounding voice with controllable style.

    Text-to-speech generates natural voice from text, with control over style and pace. Disclose when users hear AI-generated voices.

  25. Structured OutputsConstraining model output to a schema such as JSON so software can use it reliably.

    Structured outputs constrain the model to produce data in a schema such as JSON, so software can use the result reliably. Validate the output in code as well.

  26. Function CallingThe model returns a structured request to call a named function, which your code runs.

    The model returns a structured request to call a named function with arguments, and the application runs it. This lets models query databases or book appointments safely.

  27. Model Selection and BenchmarkingChoosing a model by testing it on your own task data, not only public leaderboards.

    Choose a model by testing it on your own tasks, measuring quality, latency and cost. Public leaderboards are a hint, not proof of fit for your use case.

  28. Model LatencyTime to first token and total time; shaped by model size, output length and load.

    Latency is shaped by model size, output length and load. Time to first token affects perceived speed, so streaming responses improves user experience.

  29. Model Cost and Token EconomicsPrice per input and output token, multiplied by volume; shorter prompts, caching and smaller models cut cost.

    Cost equals tokens processed multiplied by price per token. Shorter prompts, caching, smaller models for easy tasks and batching reduce cost. Track cost per successful task, not per call.

  30. LLM Limitations and HallucinationsModels can state false things confidently, lack current knowledge, and vary between runs; design for verification.

    Models can state false information confidently, lack current knowledge and vary between runs. Design for verification: show sources, check high-stakes outputs and allow users to correct errors.

    A good prompt states the task, context, constraints and output format. Clear instructions reduce variation and make outputs easier to evaluate.

  2. Prompt StructureTypical parts: role, task, context, examples, constraints and output format.

    Typical parts are role, task, context, examples, constraints and output format. Keep the order consistent so teams can read and edit prompts quickly.

    A system prompt sets persistent role, rules and behaviour for a whole conversation or product. It is the first place to put product policy, tone and safety rules.

  4. User PromptsThe per-turn request or input from the user.

    The user prompt is the per-turn request. Treat its content as untrusted input, especially when it is combined with company data.

  5. Role-Based PromptingAssigning a persona or expertise to shape tone and focus.

    Asking the model to act as an expert, such as a senior tax advisor, can shape tone and focus. It does not add knowledge the model lacks, so verify the content.

  6. Zero-Shot PromptingAsking for the task with no examples.

    Zero-shot asks for the task with no examples. It is the fastest approach and works well for common tasks with clear instructions.

  7. One-Shot PromptingProviding a single example of the desired output.

    One-shot gives a single example of the desired output. It helps the model match format or style.

  8. Few-Shot PromptingProviding several examples so the model infers the pattern.

    Few-shot provides several examples so the model infers the pattern. Choose examples that cover typical and difficult cases.

  9. Instruction-Based PromptingExplicit step-by-step instructions and rules for the task.

    Explicit step-by-step instructions and rules define the task. Numbered steps and clear do and do-not lists reduce ambiguity.

  10. Chain-of-Thought ConceptsAsking the model to reason through steps, which can improve accuracy on multi-step problems.

    Asking the model to reason through steps can improve accuracy on multi-step problems. For products, consider whether the reasoning should be shown to users or kept internal.

  11. Prompt ChainingSplitting a task into several prompts where each output feeds the next.

    A task is split into several prompts where each output feeds the next, such as extract, then validate, then summarise. Chains are easier to test than one very long prompt.

  12. Structured PromptingUsing sections, delimiters and schemas to make prompts unambiguous.

    Sections, delimiters and schemas make prompts unambiguous. Use clear markers around user-provided text so the model does not confuse it with instructions.

  13. JSON-Based OutputsRequesting JSON with defined fields and validating it in code.

    Requesting JSON with defined fields lets software use the result. Validate it in code and handle malformed output with a retry or a fallback.

  14. Prompt TemplatesReusable prompts with placeholders filled at run time.

    Templates are reusable prompts with placeholders filled at run time. They keep wording consistent across features and make changes easier to review.

  15. Dynamic Prompt VariablesInserting user, document or session data into templates safely.

    Variables insert user, document or session data into a template. Escape or delimit inserted text, so it cannot change the instructions.

  16. Context EngineeringDeciding exactly what information, tools and memory the model sees at each step.

    Context engineering decides exactly what information, tools and memory the model sees at each step. It often matters more than the wording of the prompt.

  17. Context Window OptimisationPrioritising, summarising and trimming content to fit limits while keeping what matters.

    Prioritise, summarise and trim content to fit the window while keeping what matters. Put the most important information where the model attends most reliably, usually near the instructions or the question.

  18. Memory and Context ManagementStoring and recalling conversation facts, summaries and user preferences across turns and sessions.

    Memory stores conversation facts, summaries and preferences across turns and sessions. Ask users what should be remembered and let them delete it.

  19. Prompt VersioningTracking prompt changes like code, with versions, owners and test results.

    Track prompt changes like code, with versions, owners and test results. Without versioning, teams cannot tell which change caused a quality drop.

  20. Prompt EvaluationScoring prompt versions against a fixed test set before release.

    Score prompt versions against a fixed test set before release. Use the same inputs for every version so the comparison is fair.

  21. Prompt Injection AwarenessUntrusted text can try to override instructions; treat external content as data, not commands.

    Untrusted text, including content from web pages or documents, can try to override instructions. Treat external content as data, not commands, and limit what tools the model can use.

  22. Prompt GuardrailsInput and output checks, refusals and policy rules that keep behaviour within bounds.

    Input and output checks, refusals and policy rules keep behaviour within bounds. Guardrails are layers, so combine rules, classifiers and human review for high-risk cases.

  23. Production Prompt ManagementCentral storage, review, rollout, monitoring and rollback of prompts used in live products.

    Production prompts need central storage, review, staged rollout, monitoring and rollback. Treat a prompt change like a code change with the same release discipline.

SUBJECT 14Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)29 topics

Grounding model answers in your own data: architecture, retrieval quality, evaluation and production operation.

  1. RAG FundamentalsRetrieve relevant documents at request time and give them to the model to ground its answer.

    Retrieval-augmented generation finds relevant documents at request time and gives them to the model so its answer is grounded in them. It lets a product answer from current company data without retraining the model.

  2. Why RAG Is RequiredModels lack your private or recent data and can hallucinate; RAG supplies sources and enables citations.

    Models do not know your private or recent data and can hallucinate. RAG supplies sources, which enables citations and makes answers checkable.

  3. RAG vs Fine-TuningRAG adds knowledge at query time and is easy to update; fine-tuning changes behaviour or style. They are often combined.

    RAG adds knowledge at query time and is easy to update. Fine-tuning changes behaviour, style or format. Many products start with RAG and fine-tune only if behaviour still falls short.

  4. RAG ArchitectureIngest and index, retrieve, rerank, build context, generate, cite, and monitor.

    The pipeline ingests and indexes documents, retrieves candidates for a query, reranks them, builds the context, generates the answer with citations and is monitored for quality.

  5. Data IngestionCollecting documents from sources and keeping the index in sync with changes.

    Ingestion collects documents from sources such as drives, wikis and databases, then keeps the index in sync when content changes. Stale content is a common cause of wrong answers.

  6. Document ParsingExtracting clean text, tables and structure from PDFs, web pages and office files.

    Parsing extracts clean text, tables and headings from PDFs, web pages and office files. Poor parsing silently degrades every later step, so check parsed output by hand.

  7. Data CleaningRemoving noise, duplicates and boilerplate and fixing encoding so retrieval is accurate.

    Cleaning removes boilerplate, duplicates and noise, and fixes encoding problems. Clean data makes retrieval more accurate and smaller.

  8. Document ChunkingSplitting documents into passages sized for retrieval and context limits.

    Chunking splits documents into passages sized for retrieval and the context window. Chunks that are too small lose meaning; chunks that are too large dilute relevance.

  9. Chunking StrategiesFixed size, recursive, by heading or semantic boundaries, with overlap to preserve context.

    Options include fixed-size chunks, recursive splitting, splitting by heading, and semantic boundaries, often with some overlap to keep context. Evaluate each option on your own questions.

  10. Embedding ModelsModels that convert text to vectors; choose by quality, language coverage, dimensions and cost.

    Embedding models turn text into vectors. Compare them on retrieval quality for your language and domain, plus dimensions, cost and speed.

  11. Vector DatabasesStores optimised for nearest-neighbour search over embeddings.

    Vector databases store embeddings and run fast nearest-neighbour search. They also support metadata filters. Choose based on scale, hosting needs and integration.

  12. PineconeA managed vector database service with simple scaling.

    Pinecone is a managed vector database service with simple scaling. Teams that do not want to run infrastructure often choose a managed option.

  13. WeaviateAn open-source vector database with hybrid search and modules.

    Weaviate is an open-source vector database with hybrid search and modules for embedding. It can be self-hosted or used as a managed service.

  14. ChromaA lightweight open-source vector store popular for prototypes.

    Chroma is a lightweight open-source vector store, popular for prototypes and small applications. Check its production readiness for your scale before committing.

  15. FAISSMeta's library for efficient similarity search over vectors, run in your own process.

    FAISS is a library from Meta for efficient similarity search, run inside your own process. It is fast but does not provide a full database service by itself.

  16. Semantic SearchFinding passages by meaning using embeddings, even without shared keywords.

    Semantic search finds passages by meaning, even when they do not share keywords. It handles paraphrases well but can miss exact codes and names.

  17. Keyword SearchMatching exact terms (BM25), strong for names, codes and rare words.

    Keyword search, often BM25, matches exact terms. It is strong for names, product codes and rare words that embeddings can blur.

  18. Hybrid SearchCombining keyword and semantic scores for better recall.

    Hybrid search combines keyword and semantic scores. It usually improves recall over either method alone, at the cost of more tuning.

  19. Metadata FilteringRestricting retrieval by attributes such as date, source, permission or product.

    Filters restrict retrieval by attributes such as date, source, product or user permission. Permission filters are essential so users only retrieve documents they may read.

  20. Retrieval StrategiesTop-k, multi-query, parent-document, query rewriting and filters tuned to the content.

    Options include top-k retrieval, multi-query retrieval, parent-document retrieval and query rewriting. Choose based on the failure patterns you see in evaluation.

  21. RerankingA second model reorders retrieved candidates by relevance before generation.

    A reranker, a second model, reorders retrieved candidates by relevance before generation. It improves precision at the cost of extra latency.

  22. Context ConstructionSelecting, ordering and formatting retrieved passages inside the prompt.

    Context construction selects, orders and formats retrieved passages inside the prompt, with source labels. Include only what supports the answer, since extra text can distract the model.

  23. Grounded GenerationInstructing the model to answer only from provided sources and say when they are insufficient.

    The model is instructed to answer only from the provided sources and to say when they are insufficient. Test that it does this on questions the documents do not cover.

  24. Citation and Source AttributionLinking each claim to its source so users can verify.

    Each claim links to its source so users can verify it. Citations build trust, but they must point to the passage that actually supports the claim.

  25. RAG EvaluationMeasuring retrieval and answer quality separately on a labelled question set.

    Evaluate retrieval and answer quality separately on a labelled set of questions. A wrong answer may come from retrieval or from generation, and you need to know which.

  26. Retrieval Precision and RecallPrecision is the share of retrieved passages that are relevant; recall is the share of relevant passages retrieved.

    Precision is the share of retrieved passages that are relevant. Recall is the share of relevant passages that were retrieved. Both must be measured to tune chunking and ranking.

PM frameworks cheat sheet

FrameworkUse it toHow it works
RICEPrioritise a backlogReach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. Score each idea, then sanity-check the ranking.
KanoDecide which features delightClassify features as Must-be, Performance, Attractive, Indifferent or Reverse using a short survey.
MoSCoWScope a releaseMust have, Should have, Could have, Won't have (this time).
Jobs-to-be-doneUnderstand motivationWhen [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].
AARRR (Pirate metrics)Map the growth funnelAcquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral, Revenue.
HEARTMeasure user experienceHappiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, Task success, each with goals, signals and metrics.
North Star metricAlign the teamOne metric that captures the value users get, supported by input metrics the team can move.
Five whysFind the root causeAsk 'why?' repeatedly until you reach a cause you can act on.
CIRCLESAnswer product design questionsComprehend, Identify users, Report needs, Cut through prioritisation, List solutions, Evaluate trade-offs, Summarise.

PRD template

PRD: [Feature name] Owner: [Name] · Status: Draft · Last updated: [Date] 1. Problem Who has the problem, what it is, and the evidence (data, interviews, tickets). 2. Goal and success metrics Primary metric (with baseline and target), secondary metrics, guardrails. 3. Users and use cases Target segment, top 3 user stories: "As a [user], I want [action] so that [benefit]." 4. Solution What we'll build (link designs), what's out of scope, open questions. 5. AI behaviour (if applicable) Inputs, model, prompt/approach, what it must never do, human review, evaluation set and pass criteria, cost per request. 6. Launch plan Experiment design, rollout stages, support and comms, rollback criteria. 7. Risks and dependencies Technical, legal/privacy, operational; owners and mitigations.
